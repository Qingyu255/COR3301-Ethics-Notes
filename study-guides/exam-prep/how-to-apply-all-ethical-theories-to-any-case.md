# How to Apply Every Ethical Theory to Any Case—Fast

This is a crash course in **application**, not memorization. By the end, you should be able to take an ordinary case—even “a student sleeps in class”—and produce a defensible analysis under every theory taught in Weeks 1–5.

The course theories are:

1. act utilitarianism;
2. rule utilitarianism;
3. Kantian ethics;
4. Machiavellian egoism;
5. Hobbesian egoism;
6. Randian egoism;
7. Aristotelian virtue ethics; and
8. Confucian ethics.

Weeks 3–5 do not replace these theories. They teach you to use them on environmental, advertising, business, digital, and AI cases.

---

## 1. The one mental map to remember

When you see any action or inaction, run it through five lenses:

| Lens | Fast question | Theory |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcomes** | What happens to everyone? | Act and rule utilitarianism |
| **Duty** | Could everyone do this, and are people respected? | Kant |
| **Self-interest** | How does this serve the actor? | Machiavelli, Hobbes, and Rand |
| **Character** | What virtue or vice does this express? | Aristotle |
| **Relationships** | What reciprocal role-duty applies? | Confucianism |

Memorize this sequence:

> **Outcomes → Duty → Self → Character → Relationships**

It prevents the theories from blending into one vague question about whether something “feels wrong.”

---

## 2. Before using any theory: turn the story into ethical issues

An ethical issue is a debatable **action or inaction**. It does not have to be unethical.

Use this formula:

> Whether **[actor]** acted ethically by **[physical action or omission]**.

### Good issue statements

- Whether the **student** acted ethically by **sleeping during class instead of participating**.
- Whether the **lecturer** acted ethically by **allowing the student to remain asleep**.
- Whether a **classmate** acted ethically by **recording and posting the sleeping student**.
- Whether the **university** acted ethically by **failing to provide an accommodation after receiving medical notice**.

### Bad issue statements

- “Whether the student was tired.” This is a state, not an action or inaction.
- “Whether sleep is unethical.” This is too abstract and lacks an actor and context.
- “Whether the student thought class was boring.” A thought alone is not the physical conduct being assessed.
- “Whether the situation is bad.” This does not identify who did what.

One story can contain several ethical issues. Separate them because different actors, acts, facts, and theories can produce different conclusions.

## 3. The universal six-step case routine

Write these six letters in the margin: **I–F–S–A–T–C**.

### I — Issue

Name one actor and one precise action or inaction.

### F — Facts

Separate:

- what the question states;
- what you can reasonably infer;
- what is unknown.

Do not invent convenient facts. “The student slept” does not tell us whether the cause was laziness, illness, medication, night-shift work, caregiving, or an inaccessible timetable.

### S — Stakeholders

Ask who receives the benefit, harm, risk, or burden. Include indirect and vulnerable parties where relevant.

### A — Alternatives

Compare realistic choices. For the sleeping student, alternatives may include staying awake, leaving quietly, informing the lecturer, seeking medical help, or requesting an accommodation.

### T — Theory

State the exact test, then apply **every part** of it to the facts. Do not merely name the theory.

### C — Conclusion

Give a degree and explain what fact could change it:

> The act was likely unethical to a small extent because ____. However, if ____, the judgment would change because ____.

This is better than saying only “it depends.”

## 4. Theories as eight small machines

For each theory, learn three things: its input, its test, and its output.

## 4.1 Act utilitarianism: this act's consequences

### Core test

> The act is ethical if, compared with realistic alternatives, it produces the greatest overall balance of welfare over harm for **everyone affected**.

### Run the test

1. Name the exact act.
2. List everyone affected.
3. Identify benefits and harms for each.
4. Compare likelihood, intensity, duration, scale, and reversibility.
5. Compare realistic alternatives.
6. Decide which option creates the greatest net welfare.

### What it notices

Consequences, including unintended or unknown ones. Ignorance may affect blame under another theory, but a real harm still counts in the utilitarian balance.

### Common mistake

Do not count people or consequences mechanically. A tiny convenience for 100 people does not automatically outweigh catastrophic harm to one person.

### Instant sentence starter

> Under act utilitarianism, [act] is ethical if its benefits to all affected parties outweigh its harms more than the available alternatives. Here, ____ benefits ____, while ____ harms ____. Because ____ is more likely/severe/lasting than ____, the act is ____.

---

## 4.2 Rule utilitarianism: consequences if a rule is generally accepted

### Core test

> The act is ethical if it follows the generally applicable rule whose acceptance would maximize welfare.

### Run the test

1. Formulate two or more candidate rules.
2. Imagine each rule being generally accepted in comparable cases.
3. Compare the welfare created by those rules.
4. Choose the best rule.
5. Ask whether the act follows it.

### What it notices

Trust, predictability, precedent, and repeated conduct.

### Common mistakes

- Do not use the existing law or school rule automatically; welfare must justify the rule.
- Do not write a rule tailored to one person, such as “Qingyu may sleep whenever Qingyu wants.”
- A general rule may contain a morally relevant exception, such as illness or emergency.

### Instant sentence starter

> Under rule utilitarianism, compare the rules “[rule 1]” and “[rule 2].” General acceptance of [selected rule] would create greater welfare because ____. Since [actor's] act [conforms/does not conform] to that rule, it is ____.

---

## 4.3 Kantian ethics: all three requirements

### Core test

An act is ethical only if it satisfies all three:

```text
good intention
AND universalizable maxim
AND respect for persons
```

### Run the test

1. **Good intention:** Was the actor trying to do the right thing? If the facts do not reveal intention, say so.
2. **Universalizability:** State the maxim: “When I am in circumstance C, I will do A to achieve P.” Could every relevantly similar person act on it without contradiction or self-serving double standards?
3. **Respect for persons:** Did the actor preserve other people's informed and free choice, or deceive, coerce, exploit, or use them merely as tools?

### What it notices

Motive, moral consistency, autonomy, consent, deception, coercion, and dignity.

### Common mistake

A good intention is not enough. Analyze all three requirements even when one clearly fails.

### Instant sentence starter

> Kantian ethics requires good intention, a universalizable maxim, and respect for persons. First, ____. Second, the maxim “[maxim]” [can/cannot] be universalized because ____. Third, [actor] [did/did not] respect [person] as an end because ____. Since [all requirements are met/at least one fails], the act is ____ under Kant.

---

## 4.4 Machiavellian egoism: power

### Core test

> An act is right if it is done to acquire or preserve the actor's power to dominate others.

### Run the test

1. Keep one actor fixed.
2. Identify the power, control, influence, loyalty, status, or strategic leverage gained.
3. Ask whom the actor can dominate or influence.
4. Compare short-term power with long-term backlash, resistance, punishment, or reputational loss.

### What it notices

Power and its durability.

### Common mistake

Cruelty is not automatically Machiavellian, and kindness is not automatically non-Machiavellian. Either can be ethical under this theory if it secures durable power.

### Instant sentence starter

> Under Machiavellian egoism, [act] is ethical if it acquires or preserves [actor's] power to dominate. It may strengthen power by ____, but may weaken it in the long term through ____. Therefore, it is ____ under Machiavelli.

---

## 4.5 Hobbesian egoism: self-interested cooperation

### Core test

> Cooperation is right when that cooperation advances the actor's self-interest.

### Run the test

1. Identify who cooperates with whom.
2. State what each side contributes.
3. Identify how the actor benefits.
4. Compare cooperation with conflict, refusal, or acting alone.
5. Ask whether this is genuine cooperation rather than mere coercion or collusion.

### What it notices

Mutual dependence, stable order, agreement, security, and reciprocal advantage.

### Common mistake

Do not call every group activity cooperation. A coerced victim or a group colluding to exploit others creates a serious problem for the label.

### Instant sentence starter

> Under Hobbesian egoism, cooperation is right when it serves the actor's self-interest. [Actor] cooperates with [party] by ____, gaining ____. Compared with non-cooperation, this [does/does not] protect the actor's welfare because ____. Therefore, the act is ____.

---

## 4.6 Randian egoism: rational, long-term, uncoerced self-interest

### Core test

```text
rational advancement of self-interest
AND long-term well-being or happiness
AND uncoerced personal choice
```

### Run the test

1. Identify the actor's claimed benefit.
2. Ask whether the choice was voluntary and uncoerced.
3. Distinguish a rational interest from an impulse, whim, addiction, or mistaken belief.
4. Compare immediate gratification with long-term life, happiness, and flourishing.

### What it notices

Freedom of choice and the actor's rational long-term welfare.

### Common mistake

“The actor wanted it” does not prove that it served rational self-interest.

### Instant sentence starter

> Under Randian egoism, the act must rationally and voluntarily advance the actor's long-term self-interest. Although [act] gives [short-term benefit], it [supports/undermines] long-term well-being by ____. Because the choice was [uncoerced/coerced] and [rational/irrational], it is ____.

---

## 4.7 Aristotle: voluntary action, virtue, and the mean

### Core test

> A voluntary act is ethical when it expresses a relevant virtue at the context-sensitive mean between deficiency and excess, as determined by rational judgment.

### Run the test

1. **Voluntariness first:** Was the actor ignorant of the circumstances and remorseful, or externally compelled while contributing nothing? If so, the act may be involuntary. Duress can be mixed.
2. Name the most relevant virtue: honesty, courage, justice, loyalty, moderation, compassion, diligence, practical wisdom, and so on.
3. Identify its deficiency and excess.
4. Locate the appropriate mean for this context and explain why it belongs there.
5. Ask what habit or character repeated acts would cultivate.

### What it notices

Agency, character, habit, context, and wise judgment.

### Common mistakes

- The mean is not automatically a compromise or mathematical midpoint.
- Do not judge a person's entire character from one act; assess whether this act expresses virtue while noting the limits.

### Instant sentence starter

> Aristotle first asks whether the act was voluntary. Here, ____. The relevant virtue is ____, whose deficiency is ____ and excess is ____. In this context, the rational mean requires ____ because ____. The act therefore [does/does not] express virtue and is ____.

---

## 4.8 Confucian ethics: reciprocal relationships and humane public good

### Core test

> An act is ethical when it fulfils reciprocal duties within the relevant relationship, cultivates virtue and humanity, and contributes to social harmony and public good.

The five course relationships are:

1. parent–child;
2. husband–wife;
3. elder–younger sibling;
4. ruler–subject or master–servant; and
5. elder–younger friend.

Also use the negative golden rule:

> Do not do to others what you would not want them to do to you.

### Run the test

1. Identify a listed relationship or clearly state and justify a modern analogy.
2. State the duties on **both** sides; hierarchy does not erase reciprocity.
3. Apply the negative golden rule.
4. Ask whether the conduct cultivates virtue, humanity, harmony, and public good.
5. Consider whether loyalty or harmony is concealing wrongdoing or weakening individual protection.

### What it notices

Role, hierarchy, reciprocity, loyalty, care, harmony, and public good.

### Common mistake

Do not reduce Confucianism to obedience. A person with authority also has duties of humane care.

### Instant sentence starter

> Under Confucian ethics, the relationship between [parties] is [one of the listed relationships/analogous to ____] because ____. [Actor] owes ____, while [other party] owes ____. By [doing/omitting] ____, the actor [fulfils/breaches] this reciprocal duty and [promotes/harms] harmony and public good. The act is therefore ____.

## 5. Master example: a student sleeps in class

### Minimal facts

> During a lecture, Sam falls asleep and remains asleep for twenty minutes. The question gives no reason for the tiredness and says only that Sam does not disturb anyone.

### Issue

> Whether Sam acted ethically by sleeping during the lecture instead of remaining attentive or leaving the room.

### Missing facts that matter

- Did Sam choose to stay up for entertainment, or work a night shift to survive?
- Was Sam ill, disabled, medicated, or caring for someone?
- Did sleeping disrupt anybody?
- Is attendance compulsory?
- Could Sam leave, notify the lecturer, obtain notes, or seek an accommodation?
- Is this a single incident or a repeated habit?

The facts do not justify assuming laziness. Use conditional reasoning.

### 5.1 Act utilitarianism

Sleeping may benefit Sam through urgently needed rest and may cause little direct harm if it is silent. Harms may include lost learning, wasted tuition or teaching effort, poorer group participation, and a possible effect on class morale. The available alternatives matter: leaving quietly or informing the lecturer may preserve rest while reducing disrespect or disruption.

**Likely conclusion:** If Sam is medically exhausted and sleeping quietly prevents greater harm, the act may be ethical. If Sam voluntarily stayed up for trivial reasons and repeatedly loses learning, the small immediate comfort is unlikely to outweigh the longer-term harms, making it unethical.

### 5.2 Rule utilitarianism

Compare:

- Rule A: “Students may sleep in class whenever they feel tired.”
- Rule B: “Students should remain attentive, but may rest or leave when illness, disability, or serious exhaustion makes participation unreasonable, while taking reasonable steps to catch up.”

General acceptance of Rule A may undermine learning and the classroom environment. Rule B preserves education while allowing proportionate exceptions and likely creates greater welfare.

**Likely conclusion:** Sam's act is ethical if it falls within Rule B's justified exception and Sam takes reasonable steps to catch up; otherwise, it is likely unethical.

### 5.3 Kantian ethics

**Good intention:** Unknown. Sam may be protecting health, or may simply be avoiding effort. State the uncertainty.

**Universalizability:** A maxim such as “Whenever remaining awake is inconvenient, I will sleep through class while expecting teaching resources to remain available” is difficult to universalize because widespread adoption undermines the educational practice it relies on. A narrower but generally applicable maxim—“When illness makes participation unsafe or impossible, a student may rest and later make up the work”—is more defensible.

**Respect for persons:** Quiet sleep is not automatically deception, coercion, or using someone merely as a means. However, deliberately treating the lecturer's effort as disposable while expecting continued support may show inadequate respect. If Sam communicates a medical need and minimizes disruption, respect is more likely preserved.

**Likely conclusion:** On the minimal facts, Kant's result is indeterminate because intention is unknown and respect is debatable. A health-based, universalizable exception may be ethical; casual free-riding likely fails at least universalizability and possibly respect.

### 5.4 Machiavellian egoism

Ordinary sleeping gives Sam no clear power to dominate others, so Machiavelli is a weak fit. If Sam deliberately sleeps to display defiance, gain status among classmates, or weaken the lecturer's authority, it may create short-term influence. Yet punishment and reputational loss may reduce Sam's long-term power.

**Likely conclusion:** On the given facts, there is insufficient evidence that the act pursues power, so the theory gives no strong ethical justification. If it is an unsuccessful display of dominance, it is unethical under Machiavelli's own test.

### 5.5 Hobbesian egoism

A class can be understood as a cooperative arrangement: the lecturer prepares and teaches, while students attend and participate so that all gain from a functioning learning environment. Sleeping may amount to free-riding if Sam receives the benefits of the class without contributing the expected attention or participation. If the class permits quiet rest as part of an accommodation and Sam later catches up, the conduct can remain within a stable cooperative scheme.

**Likely conclusion:** Unexplained repeated sleeping that weakens the cooperative arrangement is likely unethical. An agreed health accommodation that protects both Sam and the class may be ethical.

### 5.6 Randian egoism

The question is whether sleeping is Sam's rational, voluntary route to long-term flourishing. Rest may preserve health and future learning, so it can serve rational self-interest. But momentary comfort that damages grades, education, reputation, or future opportunity may be an irrational trade of long-term welfare for short-term gratification. If illness overwhelms Sam involuntarily, the act may not be a genuinely chosen Randian action at all.

**Likely conclusion:** Necessary rest may be ethical when it rationally protects Sam's long-term well-being. Repeated avoidable sleeping that sabotages education is likely unethical.

### 5.7 Aristotle

Start with voluntariness. If medication or illness caused Sam to fall asleep without meaningful control, blaming Sam's character is difficult. If the choice was voluntary, relevant virtues include diligence, respect, moderation, and practical wisdom.

```text
deficiency: apathy or laziness
mean: attentive participation balanced with responsible care for health
excess: forcing oneself to remain awake despite serious illness or dangerous exhaustion
```

The mean is not “sleep for exactly ten minutes.” It is the context-sensitive response a practically wise student would take—perhaps notifying the lecturer, resting when genuinely necessary, and catching up later.

**Likely conclusion:** Avoidable sleeping can express a deficiency of diligence. Necessary rest managed responsibly may express moderation and practical wisdom.

### 5.8 Confucian ethics

Teacher–student is not one of the five relationships listed in the course, so state an analogy rather than inventing a sixth traditional relationship. It may be analogized to an elder–younger or ruler–subject relationship because it involves unequal experience, guidance, authority, and reciprocal responsibilities.

Sam may owe respect, sincere effort, and receptiveness to teaching. The lecturer owes humane care, appropriate guidance, and attention to a student's welfare. Sleeping without explanation may breach Sam's role-duty and weaken classroom harmony. But humiliating or punishing an ill student without inquiry may breach the lecturer's reciprocal duty.

**Likely conclusion:** Avoidable disengagement is likely unethical, while health-related sleep handled through honest communication may be compatible with reciprocal care. Confucianism also reminds us to assess the lecturer's response as a separate ethical act.

## 6. What the sleeping case teaches

The same physical conduct can receive different judgments because each theory asks a different question:

| Theory | Decisive fact in the sleeping case |
|---|---|
| Act utilitarianism | actual and expected benefits and harms |
| Rule utilitarianism | which general attendance rule maximizes welfare |
| Kant | intention, maxim, and respect for persons |
| Machiavelli | whether sleeping builds durable power |
| Hobbes | whether it supports or free-rides on cooperation |
| Rand | whether it rationally serves voluntary long-term self-interest |
| Aristotle | voluntariness and the virtuous mean between neglect and unhealthy excess |
| Confucianism | reciprocal teacher–student duties and harmony |

Do not force every theory to reach the same answer. Disagreement shows that you have preserved the theories' distinct tests.

Do not force a theory when the facts do not activate it. Saying “Machiavelli is a weak fit because no pursuit of power is shown” is better than inventing domination.

## 7. How to choose theories quickly in an exam

If the question requires only selected theories, use factual triggers:

| If the case emphasizes... | Reach for... |
|---|---|
| many stakeholders, risk, policy, trade-offs, or uncertain harm | act utilitarianism |
| repeated conduct, precedent, trust, or institutional rules | rule utilitarianism |
| deception, coercion, consent, double standards, motive, or dignity | Kant |
| control, influence, rivalry, or political strategy | Machiavelli |
| teamwork, contracts, mutual dependence, security, or order | Hobbes |
| free choice, personal welfare, addiction, survival, or long-term benefit | Rand |
| habit, character, courage, honesty, moderation, duress, or judgment | Aristotle |
| family, hierarchy, loyalty, favours, care, employer–employee relations, or harmony | Confucianism |

Choose theories for fit, not because one sounds more sophisticated. Act and rule utilitarianism are two versions of one family; unless the instructions say otherwise, do not assume they count as two different theories for a minimum-theory requirement.

## 9. One paragraph formula that works for every theory

Use **D–A–C–L–J**:

1. **Doctrine:** State the theory's full test.
2. **Application:** Connect each required element to a specific fact.
3. **Counterargument:** Give the strongest opposing reading.
4. **Limitation:** Explain one theory-specific uncertainty or weakness that matters here.
5. **Judgment:** Give a clear result and degree.

### Model paragraph

> Aristotle's virtue ethics first requires a voluntary act, then asks whether it expresses the context-sensitive mean of a relevant virtue. Sam's choice appears voluntary only if tiredness did not remove meaningful control. The relevant virtue is diligence: habitual disengagement is a deficiency, while forcing oneself to remain in class despite dangerous exhaustion may be an unhealthy excess. The rational mean may be to inform the lecturer, leave quietly, rest, and catch up. A counterargument is that remaining physically present causes no disruption, but that does not by itself show practical wisdom or responsible learning. Because the facts do not explain Sam's condition, the judgment is conditional: avoidable sleeping is likely unethical to a moderate extent, while medically necessary rest handled responsibly may be ethical.

## 10. Common theory-mixing errors

| Mistake | Why it is wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| “Kant says the act is wrong because it caused harm.” | Consequences are not Kant's decisive test. | Analyze intention, universalizability, and respect. |
| “Utilitarianism says it is wrong because it is unfair.” | Fairness needs a welfare connection under utilitarianism. | Explain whose welfare is affected and how. |
| “Egoism says do whatever you want.” | The strands define self-interest differently. | Name Machiavelli, Hobbes, or Rand and apply that test. |
| “Aristotle says choose the middle option.” | The mean is context-sensitive, not arithmetic. | Name a virtue, deficiency, excess, and justified mean. |
| “Confucianism says obey authority.” | Duties are reciprocal. | State what both superior and subordinate owe. |
| “Rule utilitarianism means obey school rules.” | Existing rules may not maximize welfare. | Compare candidate rules under general acceptance. |
| “Four theories say unethical, so the result is 4–1.” | Ethical theories are not votes. | Compare fit, completeness, evidence, and moral seriousness. |

## 11. A ten-minute transfer drill

### New case

> A classmate notices that Sam is asleep, records a video without waking Sam, and posts it to a large group chat as a joke.

Before opening the answer, try to write one line for each lens:

- Outcomes:
- Duty:
- Self-interest—power, cooperation, and rational long-term welfare:
- Character:
- Relationships:

<details>
<summary>Suggested analysis</summary>

**Act utilitarianism:** Brief amusement must be balanced against humiliation, privacy loss, bullying, damaged trust, and permanent circulation. Asking permission or making a joke without recording is a less harmful alternative. Posting is likely unethical.

**Rule utilitarianism:** General acceptance of “students may record and post vulnerable classmates for amusement without consent” would undermine trust and create widespread harm. A consent-based rule likely maximizes welfare. The post is unethical.

**Kant:** The intention may be amusement rather than direct harm, but the maxim is difficult to universalize. Using Sam's vulnerable state as content without consent treats Sam merely as a means to entertainment. The act is unethical.

**Machiavelli:** The classmate may gain short-term attention or social influence. Backlash, discipline, and lost trust may weaken durable power. The outcome depends on the power effects, illustrating the theory's moral narrowness.

**Hobbes:** Posting erodes the trust required for classmates to cooperate safely. A shared norm against non-consensual recording protects each person's self-interest. The act is likely unethical.

**Rand:** The voluntary post may give momentary pleasure, but disciplinary, reputational, and relational costs may make it irrational for long-term flourishing. It is likely unethical.

**Aristotle:** The act is voluntary and may show deficiencies of compassion, respect, and practical wisdom, possibly an excess of attention-seeking humour. It is likely unethical.

**Confucianism:** Between classmates, use a justified elder–younger friend analogy only if the facts support different seniority; otherwise emphasize reciprocal restraint and humane social harmony without pretending there is a perfect listed relationship. Posting breaches reciprocity and harms harmony. It is likely unethical.

</details>

## 12. Final self-test

If you can answer these from memory, you can apply the theories:

1. What is the difference between act and rule utilitarianism?
2. What are Kant's three cumulative requirements?
3. What does each egoist mean by self-interest?
4. What must Aristotle assess before virtue?
5. Why is Aristotle's mean not simply a compromise?
6. What makes Confucian duties reciprocal?
7. How do you identify an ethical issue?
8. What should you do when the facts do not reveal intention, harm, or voluntariness?

<details>
<summary>Answer key</summary>

1. Act utilitarianism judges this act's consequences; rule utilitarianism selects the generally accepted welfare-maximizing rule and tests whether the act follows it.
2. Good intention, universalizable maxim, and respect for persons.
3. Machiavelli: durable power to dominate. Hobbes: beneficial cooperation. Rand: rational, long-term, uncoerced self-interest.
4. Whether the act was voluntary.
5. The mean is the context-sensitive expression of a virtue determined by rational judgment, not an arithmetic midpoint.
6. Both sides of a hierarchical relationship owe duties; authority is not unconditional permission and subordination is not blind obedience.
7. Name a specific actor and a debatable physical action or omission. The conduct need not already be unethical.
8. State the uncertainty, give conditional analyses, and explain why the missing fact changes the result. Do not invent it.

</details>
