## 1. The map: what question does each theory ask?

Each ethical theory asks a different question about the same event and foregrounds different moral considerations. The theories are not arranged as a ladder in which each new theory replaces the previous one, and the course does not present a single framework as the automatic answer to every case.

| Theory | Its main question | What it foregrounds |
|---|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | Which option produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone? | Consequences |
| Kantian ethics | Was the act well-intentioned, universalizable, and respectful of persons? | Intention and duty |
| Ethical egoism | Does the act advance the actor's self-interest in the way this strand requires? | Self-interest |
| Aristotelian virtue ethics | Does the act express a cultivated virtue in the appropriate measure? | Character and flourishing |
| Confucianism | Does the act fulfil reciprocal duties within the relevant relationships? | Relationships and social roles |

The course is designed not only to teach these theories but to make you identify ethical dilemmas and apply theories to decisions. The assessments therefore test application rather than memorization. [W1, slides 7–8 and 18–21](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

# Week 1

## 2. Ethical reasoning is more than personal opinion

An ethical judgment is a claim about what someone **ought** to do. That makes it normative. A claim about what happened, what people prefer, or what an action caused is descriptive.

Example:

- Descriptive: “Deploying the update will expose 2% of users to data loss.”
- Normative: “The team ought to delay deployment.”
- Hidden value premise: “Avoiding serious, involuntary harm is more important than meeting this release date.”

The slides reject the idea that ethics is merely a collection of opinions or a person's preferences. The examples of stealing bread, cheating, and littering show why “my conscience says so” is not yet a reasoned analysis. [W1, slides 40–43](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

### A reusable analysis stack

For any case, separate these layers:

1. **Facts:** What does the case actually state?
2. **Ethical issue:** “Whether X's act of doing Y was ethical.”
3. **Stakeholders:** Who is directly or indirectly affected?
4. **Empirical assumptions:** What consequences or intentions are uncertain?
5. **Theory:** What exact doctrine and elements apply?
6. **Application:** What does each element reveal?
7. **Objection or limitation:** What does the theory miss or leave uncertain?
8. **Conclusion and recommendation:** What should be done, and why?

The lecturer repeatedly warned against silently changing the hypothetical. A plausible consequence is not automatically a fact. If an assumption is needed, label it and, where useful, analyze both branches: “If A, then...; if not A, then....” This is especially important for utilitarian consequences and Kantian intentions. (Week 1 transcript, 00:00:06–00:01:04; Week 2 transcript, 00:15:21–00:15:50.)

### Understanding check 1

A case states only: “A developer copied proprietary code to finish a project.” Which sentence is the best start?

A. “The copying harmed the company and caused layoffs.”  
B. “Whether the developer's copying of proprietary code was ethical.”  
C. “The developer is clearly a bad person.”

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

**B.** It identifies the act and frames the ethical issue without inventing consequences or judging the person's whole character. A adds unstated facts; C leaps to a conclusion.

</details>

## 3. Utilitarianism: seek the greatest overall welfare

### Central idea

Utilitarianism is a **teleological** theory: an act's rightness is determined by the end or consequence it produces, rather than its cause. The course doctrine is:

> An action is right if and only if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone.

[W1, slides 44–49](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

The attraction of the theory is its impartial concern for overall welfare. Its difficulty is that pleasure, pain, dignity, grief, and risk do not come with a reliable common unit, and people may disagree about which consequences should be included.

### The four conjunctive features

All four are part of the same doctrine:

| Feature | Meaning | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| **Consequentialism** | Judge options by their consequences and compare alternative actions. | What would each available action cause? |
| **Hedonism** | Pleasure is the good; avoiding pain can count as producing greater pleasure. | Who gains pleasure or avoids pain? |
| **Maximalism** | Choose the option with the greatest net pleasure, not merely some positive result. | Is this the best balance among the alternatives? |
| **Universalism** | Count everyone's pleasure and pain; the actor's interests have no extra weight. | Have all affected parties been considered impartially? |

[W1, slides 48–54 and summary diagram on slide 76](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

Notice two common mistakes:

- “This option helps somebody” is insufficient. **Maximalism** demands comparison with other options.
- “This option helps me most” is insufficient. **Universalism** gives the actor's welfare the same weight as others', not more.

Maximalism requires comparison with **feasible** alternatives, not every imaginable possibility. To show that an act is not utility-maximizing, it is enough to establish that at least one available alternative produces a better balance. To recommend the best policy, however, compare the principal realistic options rather than using an artificially narrow choice such as “do this or do nothing.”

In a timed application answer, the exact doctrine and complete analysis matter more than mechanically defining all four labels. Name and explain consequentialism, hedonism, maximalism, and universalism when the prompt asks for theory explanation or when a distinction does real work in the case.

The slides introduce cost-benefit analysis and shadow pricing as attempts to quantify utility. Money makes unlike options comparable, but this is a measurement choice, not proof that every human value has a monetary equivalent. [W1, slide 53](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

### Understanding check 2

A hospital chooses treatment A because it helps 70 patients. Treatment B would help 65 patients but would produce much larger health improvements and far less suffering. Which utilitarian feature shows why “70 is larger than 65” does not settle the case?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

**Maximalism**, together with hedonism. Utilitarianism compares the total balance and intensity of pleasure and pain, not merely the number of beneficiaries. We would still need evidence about every affected party and all feasible alternatives.

</details>

## 4. Act utilitarianism: evaluate this particular act

Act utilitarianism asks whether the specific act, in its actual or reasonably anticipated circumstances, produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone. [W1, slides 55–62](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** Reproduce the doctrine, then show the steps explicitly: identify the act, identify “everyone,” set out pleasurable and painful consequences, and state which side outweighs the other. “Everyone” was singled out as important. Do not decide the balance merely by counting the number of consequences; their nature and intensity matter. (Week 1 transcript, 00:06:31–00:07:52, 00:26:58–00:27:20, and 00:37:18–00:38:55.)

### Application steps

```text
1. Identify the precise act.
2. Identify everyone affected.
3. For each party, identify pleasurable and painful consequences.
4. Compare the net balance against the available alternatives.
5. Conclude, while marking uncertainty and assumptions.
```

### Worked example: Nick ran a stop light

The sentence alone is under-specified. If Nick is a police officer responding to a murder, the benefit of arriving sooner might outweigh the traffic risk. If Nick is drunk and strikes a pedestrian, pain overwhelmingly dominates. Act utilitarianism can therefore return different judgments for outwardly similar acts because their consequences differ. [W1, slides 57–62](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

Do not invent a collision just to make the answer easy. You may say, “Assuming other road users were present, Nick created a risk of injury,” because risk follows plausibly from the act; distinguish that from asserting that someone was actually injured.

### Understanding check 3

An engineer disables a safety alarm for ten minutes to prevent a costly shutdown. No incident occurs. Name two groups that should be considered, one missing fact that matters, and the utilitarian comparison.

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Possible groups include workers exposed to risk, customers, the company, and people who depend on the facility. A crucial missing fact is the probability and severity of harm during those ten minutes. The analysis compares the avoided shutdown costs and other benefits against increased expected harm, anxiety, precedent, and all feasible safer alternatives. “No incident occurred” does not by itself prove the decision maximized welfare, especially if this was a decision under uncertainty.

</details>

## 5. Rule utilitarianism: evaluate the generally accepted rule

Rule utilitarianism asks whether the act conforms to a rule whose **general acceptance** would produce the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone. Neither act nor rule utilitarianism is inherently “better”; use the version most relevant to the facts. [W1, slides 55–56 and 63–74](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** The lecturer expressly called it important that neither version is better or more impressive merely because rule utilitarianism looks harder. Under rule utilitarianism, test candidate rules, select the rule whose general acceptance maximizes utility, and then apply that rule to the act. A sensible rule is not marked down for being “boring” or resembling existing law. (Week 1 transcript, 00:05:26–00:05:41, 00:28:11–00:32:59, and 00:42:26–00:42:47.)

### Application steps

```text
1. Identify the act.
2. Formulate a relevant rule based on the act.
3. Model the pleasurable and painful effects if that rule were generally accepted.
4. Compare candidate rules and select the utility-maximizing one.
5. Ask whether the act conforms to that rule.
```

For Nick, “All vehicles may run stop lights” likely produces chaos and injury if generally accepted. “Vehicles must not run stop lights” is much more likely to maximize welfare. Since Nick's act violates the selected rule, rule utilitarianism judges it unethical. [W1, slides 65–74](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

A rule may contain a morally relevant exception. For example, “Vehicles must not run stop lights except during a genuine emergency and with reasonable precautions” can be tested against both an absolute prohibition and a permissive rule. The exception must be general enough to apply to comparable cases; it cannot be written around Nick merely to guarantee a preferred answer.

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** The lecturer expressly allowed conditional rules, including an emergency-vehicle exception. However, she also identified overly specific rules as a criticism because a writer can manipulate the rule until it produces a desired conclusion. Formulate a relevant, generally applicable rule and explain why its general acceptance maximizes welfare. (Week 1 transcript, 00:40:43–00:42:26.)

### Act versus rule

| | Act utilitarianism | Rule utilitarianism |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of evaluation | This act in this situation | A generally accepted rule, then the act's conformity |
| Strength | Sensitive to exceptional circumstances | Promotes stability, predictability, and efficient recurring decisions |
| Risk | Can justify harmful exceptions when the local payoff looks positive | A rule can be framed too broadly or rigidly |

Act utilitarianism assesses the particular decision in its circumstances; rule utilitarianism first asks what standing principle should govern comparable cases. Both remain consequentialist because welfare ultimately justifies the judgment.

### Understanding check 4

A student says, “My cheating is ethical under rule utilitarianism because the rule ‘Students may cheat when I personally need a higher grade’ benefits me.” What are the two errors?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

First, the rule is tailored to privilege one actor rather than being assessed under **general acceptance**. Second, it counts the student's welfare more heavily than everyone else's, violating universalism. A relevant candidate rule would apply to comparable students in comparable circumstances, and its effects on trust, grading, classmates, instructors, and institutions would need comparison with alternative rules.

</details>

## 6. Limits of utilitarianism

The slides raise five connected problems:

- Who exactly counts as “everyone”?
- Can total utility really be calculated?
- Does every consequence have a discernible monetary value?
- Are pleasure and pain subjective?
- Can their intensity across different persons be compared?

[W1, slide 75](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

The deepest measurement problem is not merely insufficient data. Interpersonal comparison may lack a common scale: one person's minor convenience and another's severe grief are not obviously commensurable. A further implication—not a separate criticism expressly listed on slide 75—is that aggregation can obscure distribution. The same total utility could be achieved by moderate benefits to many people or huge benefits to a majority paired with devastating harm to a minority.

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** Contextualized shortcomings—not a pasted list—were described as the move that can lift an answer beyond competent application. Connect the limitation to the particular facts and explain how it affects the conclusion. (Week 1 transcript, 00:36:17–00:36:34 and 00:40:02–00:40:27.)

### Understanding check 5

Suppose policy X creates +1 unit for 1,000 people and −900 units for one person. Policy Y creates +0.2 units for all 1,001 people. What does a simple sum say, and what ethical concern might that sum hide?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

On those stipulated numbers, X totals +100 while Y totals +200.2, so Y wins. More importantly, the exercise exposes two problems: the units may be fictional or incomparable, and aggregation can hide how burdens and benefits are distributed. Even if X had the larger total, we would still want to ask whether imposing catastrophic harm on one person is ethically defensible.

</details>

## 7. Kantian ethics: intentions, universal law, and human dignity

Kantian ethics shifts attention away from outcomes. Moral obligations apply regardless of consequences and arise from moral law binding rational beings. The course doctrine is:

> An action is right if it is done with good intentions and satisfies both categorical imperatives: universalisability and respect for persons.

[W2, slides 3–8](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

Treat these as three cumulative requirements:

```text
ethical under Kant = good intention
                     AND universalizable maxim
                     AND respect for persons
```

Even if one element clearly fails, the lecturer recommends analyzing all three for completeness and to expose possible criticisms of the theory.

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** Treat Kant as three separate required checks—good intention, universalizability, and respect for persons. If one fails, do **not** stop. State the failure, then continue “for the sake of completeness” through the other two. If intention is unknown, neutral, or mixed, highlight that uncertainty rather than inventing it. (Week 2 transcript, 00:00:07–00:01:36 and 00:11:21–00:13:15.)

### 7.1 Good intention

The actor must intend to do the right thing. A fortunate outcome cannot cleanse a bad intention. Conversely, the course material notes a difficulty: neutral intentions and uncertainty about what counts as “good” make application hard.

Example: a company donates to charity solely to manipulate customers. The donation may help people, but the profit-seeking or deceptive intention could fail this element, depending on how the facts support that interpretation.

### 7.2 Universalizability

A **maxim** is the rule implicit in the action. Ask whether you could rationally will everyone in relevantly similar circumstances to follow it. This counters special pleading and double standards: “I may lie when convenient, but others must tell me the truth.” [W2, slides 8–11](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

Test:

1. State the maxim without naming or privileging the actor.
2. Universalize it to relevantly similar persons and circumstances.
3. Ask whether the practice remains rational and non-self-contradictory.

If everyone may make false promises whenever useful, trust in promising collapses; the maxim undermines the practice it relies on. Be careful not to make the maxim artificially narrow merely so it passes.

### 7.3 Respect for persons

Treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never **merely** as a means. People possess inherent dignity and a capacity to choose freely; do not reduce them to resources, statistics, or tools. [W2, slides 8 and 12–13](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

Using someone's service is not automatically wrong. Hiring an engineer uses their skill as a means, but fair pay, informed consent, and freedom to refuse also recognize them as an end. Deception, coercion, or concealing material risk commonly prevents meaningful choice and treats the person merely as an instrument.

### Kant's strengths and limits

Kant protects individuals against being sacrificed merely because doing so raises aggregate welfare. But the slides identify problems: the demanding role of rationality and conflicts between perfect duties, such as honesty versus avoiding harm. Also remember: not all rational actions are moral, although the slides state that all moral actions are rational. [W2, slides 14–15](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** “Rational” and “moral” must not be used interchangeably; the lecturer identified this as feedback from a previous exam. (Week 2 transcript, 00:28:12–00:28:40.)

### Understanding check 6

A product manager hides a known security flaw so users will adopt an app. Adoption funds future security work, and no breach occurs. Apply all three Kantian elements.

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

- **Intention:** Funding security is arguably good, but intentionally hiding the flaw also suggests deception; state the ambiguity rather than assuming it away.
- **Universalizability:** A maxim permitting firms to conceal known risks to secure adoption undermines trustworthy disclosure and is difficult to will universally.
- **Respect for persons:** Users cannot give informed consent because material information is hidden; they are used merely as a means to finance the firm's plan.

The lack of a breach does not rescue the act because Kantian ethics does not make consequences decisive.

</details>

## 8. Ethical egoism: three accounts of self-interest

Ethical egoism is **normative**: it says individuals **ought** to act in self-interested ways. “Humans often pursue self-interest” is only a descriptive psychological claim and does not by itself establish what they ought to do. [W2, slides 16–18](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

The three strands define self-interest differently:

| Strand | Course doctrine | Diagnostic question | Typical concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Machiavelli** | An act is right if done to dominate, or maintain the power to dominate, others. | Does it acquire or preserve power, including in the long term? | Assumes mutual animosity and may make trust difficult. |
| **Hobbes** | Cooperation is right when that cooperation is in the actor's self-interest. | Does enforced or strategic cooperation protect long-term welfare? | Is social collapse really the alternative to self-interest? |
| **Ayn Rand** | An act is right if it rationally advances one's self-interest. | Is it rational, long-term, voluntary, and chosen without coercion? | Why should universal self-interest reliably yield prosperity under scarcity? |

[W2, slides 18–28](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** Know the distinctions among all three strands and reproduce the applicable doctrine. Choose the most applicable theory, not the theory that appears hardest. Complete the basic analysis before adding real-world commentary or disagreement. (Week 2 transcript, 00:29:05–00:30:33, 00:34:46–00:36:34, and 00:55:32–00:55:38.)

### Do egoists ever help others?

Yes. Self-interest does not mean acting alone or producing no benefit for others. A Machiavellian may donate to gain influence; a Hobbesian may cooperate to secure order; a Randian may help because doing so is a freely chosen source of happiness. The motive and structure of benefit matter. [W2, slide 27](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

For **Rand**, two conjunctive points must be kept together: people should act in self-interest, and self-interest is happiness achieved through uncoerced personal choice. The lecturer also emphasized rationality, preservation of one's life, and long-term well-being. A whim, instinct, coerced act, or obviously self-destructive choice does not automatically qualify merely because the actor calls it self-interested. (Week 2 transcript, 00:43:04–00:43:36 and 00:49:56–00:50:45.)

### Same action, different egoist analyses

Suppose an employee joins a difficult group project:

- **Machiavelli:** ethical if participation builds influence or preserves power.
- **Hobbes:** ethical if cooperation helps secure the shared grade and therefore the employee's own interest.
- **Rand:** ethical if the employee rationally and freely chooses it for long-term flourishing; coercion is a serious problem.

### Understanding check 7

A CEO funds employee education. Give one explanation under each strand that could make the same act ethical.

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

- **Machiavelli:** education increases employee loyalty and the CEO's organizational power.
- **Hobbes:** employer and employees cooperate because a more capable workforce benefits both and stabilizes the firm.
- **Rand:** investing in talent is a rational, voluntarily chosen way to advance the firm's and CEO's long-term interest.

The fact that employees benefit does not disqualify the act under egoism.

</details>

## 9. Aristotelian virtue ethics: character, habit, and judgment

Virtue ethics asks what kind of person one is becoming. A **virtue** is a character trait manifested in habitual action. Courage, honesty, justice, loyalty, moderation, and compassion are examples. Virtues require practice, are acquired, become habits, and are admired in others. [W2, slides 29–34](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

One correct act does not by itself establish a virtuous character. Aristotle's emphasis is on stable dispositions cultivated through repeated, deliberate action, while the course permits you to focus on whether the particular act in the hypothetical was virtuous.

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** Keep the recognized virtues available in your open-book notes; the lecturer said they need not be memorized but should be referred to in an answer. For exam hypotheticals, assess whether the particular act is virtuous rather than attempting to judge the actor's complete lifetime. (Week 2 transcript, 01:02:11–01:02:30 and 01:05:25–01:06:23.)

Aristotle's focus is human happiness or flourishing across a complete life. Wealth is a means to that end, not the whole of it. For the course's case analyses, however, the lecturer permits focusing on whether the particular action was virtuous. [W2, slides 34–36 and 52–53](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

### Aristotle's definition, decomposed

> Virtue is a “purposive disposition, lying in a mean that is relative to us and determined by a rational principle.”

[W2, slides 37–48](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

#### A. Purposive disposition: voluntariness is a gate

Before evaluating virtue, ask whether the act was voluntary. The slides treat an act as involuntary when:

- it results from ignorance of the circumstances and object of the act, followed by pain and remorse; or
- it occurs under external compulsion and the agent contributes nothing.

Duress is harder. Choosing a dishonorable act to save one's family may mix external pressure with agency. If the act is involuntary, the actor should not be judged vicious merely because harm occurred. [W2, slides 39–42](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** Start an Aristotelian answer by asking whether the act was voluntary. The lecturer presented this as the exam consequence of “purposive disposition,” not as an optional afterthought. (Week 2 transcript, 01:07:01–01:08:26.)

#### B. Mean: appropriate measure, not arithmetic average

Virtue lies between excess and deficiency relative to the context:

```text
deficiency        mean appropriate here        excess
cowardice   ←────────── courage ──────────→   rashness
```

The mean is not always the midpoint. A safety engineer may need unusual firmness during an emergency but more patience during routine review. Aristotle advises avoiding extremes, learning from error, and guarding against pleasures that induce vice, but does not give a precise formula. [W2, slides 44–46](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

#### C. Relative to us, rationally determined

Context changes what proportionate courage, pride, loyalty, or candor requires. Rational judgment is supposed to temper subjectivity and bias. This flexibility makes virtue ethics potentially useful in business, where judgment is unavoidable, but it also creates uncertainty about whose standard defines the mean. [W2, slides 47–54](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

The slides also say adultery, theft, and murder are evil in themselves and not eligible for a virtuous “mean,” while later criticism asks whether theft with a virtuous purpose challenges that classification. Treat that tension as an explicit course debate, not something to silently resolve. [W2, slides 49 and 54](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** The lecturer identified Aristotle's failure to specify the “sweet spot” as an exam-ready criticism. For additional credit, do not merely say the mean is subjective—explain how and why you located the mean where you did in this context. (Week 2 transcript, 01:12:59–01:13:15 and 01:18:14–01:18:29.)

### Understanding check 8

A junior engineer discovers a serious defect but stays silent because a manager threatens to end their career. How would Aristotle's analysis begin, and what virtues or extremes follow?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Begin with **voluntariness**: the threat creates duress, but the engineer may still retain some agency, so the classification is contestable. Then examine courage, honesty, loyalty, and practical judgment. Silence may be a deficiency of courage, but reckless disclosure without verifying facts or using safe escalation channels could be rash. The context-sensitive mean might involve documenting evidence and escalating through protected channels. Aristotle's limited guidance about locating that mean is a relevant criticism.

</details>

## 10. Confucianism: ethics through reciprocal relationships

Confucianism begins from the idea that humans are social creatures. Happiness involves understanding one's proper place and duties to others and cultivating oneself into a “person of excellence.” Community structure therefore brings reciprocal duties and personal compromise. [W2, slides 55–56](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

### Five central relationships

1. Parent–child
2. Husband–wife
3. Elder–younger sibling
4. Ruler–subject / master–servant
5. Elder–younger friend

These are hierarchical and recognize natural inequality, but duties exist on both sides. **Reciprocity does not mean equality.** [W2, slide 57 and slide 61](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed exam signal:** Reciprocity and the five relationships were expressly called very important. The lecturer said the parent–child relationship is paramount and stressed that hierarchical relationships impose duties on both sides. (Week 2 transcript, 01:20:22–01:23:44.)

### The negative golden rule

> Do not do unto others what you do not want others to do unto you.

The rule asks a superior not to reproduce mistreatment toward inferiors and an inferior not to reproduce it toward superiors. It is reciprocal restraint, not a command that every role become identical. [W2, slides 58–59](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

### Kinship-based subjectivity

The sheep example is deliberately uncomfortable: Confucius describes uprightness as a father concealing a son's misconduct and a son concealing a father's. Familial obligations can therefore qualify more general reciprocity and create “kinship-based subjectivity.” [W2, slides 60–61](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

This contrasts sharply with Kantian universalizability. Kant resists special exceptions for relatives; the Confucian account presented here gives family relationships special moral weight.

### Business application

Confucianism does not condemn business, but profit accumulation cannot be its sole aim. Business should cultivate virtue, humanity, self-development, and public good. The slides illustrate quasi-family company relations in Japan and reciprocal favours in China, while also asking whether exclusion from decisions or favour obligations are ethical. [W2, slides 62–66](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

When applying Confucianism to employer–employee relations, explicitly state that you are **analogizing** them to ruler–subject or master–servant relations and justify the analogy. Confucius did not list the modern employment relationship, and the analogy has limits. [W2, slide 57](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

> **Transcript-confirmed likely exam topic:** The lecturer said employer–employee relationships would “probably come out.” Because employment is not one of the five listed relationships, write expressly: “The employer–employee relationship is analogous to the master–servant relationship,” then explain the similarity and the analogy's limits. Do not attribute a modern employment doctrine directly to Confucius. (Week 2 transcript, 01:23:44–01:24:56.)

### Rights criticism

The slides characterize Confucian ethics as communitarian and lacking a doctrine of individual rights. A person may say that another failed a duty, but not necessarily claim a personal right was violated. This may preserve social harmony, yet it risks providing insufficient protection or dignity to individuals harmed by powerful people or institutions. [W2, slides 67–68](<../../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

For exam selection, Confucianism is easiest to spot when the facts contain a listed relationship, an employer–employee analogy, reciprocal favours, hierarchy, or a conflict between family loyalty and wider duties. High-value criticisms include weak protection of individual rights and the possibility that concealing family misconduct is treated as virtuous. (Week 2 transcript, 01:26:15–01:27:50 and 01:40:09–01:40:51.)

### Understanding check 9

A company plans layoffs without consulting long-serving employees. Apply the Confucian framework and give one criticism.

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

Explicitly analogize employer–employee to master–servant or ruler–subject because both involve hierarchical, ongoing dependence. Reciprocal duties could require employee loyalty and responsible work, while requiring the employer to care for employees, communicate respectfully, and avoid treating profit as the sole goal. Sudden layoffs without consultation may breach those duties. A criticism is that the framework's hierarchy and weak account of individual rights may not adequately protect workers or give them decision-making power.

</details>

# 11. One case through the five theoretical families

**Case:** A health-tech company discovers that its diagnostic model is less accurate for a minority group. It delays disclosure to protect a major funding round while engineers quietly improve the model.

| Lens | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | Compare health harms, lost trust, funding and future product improvements for every affected group. Act utilitarianism examines this delay; rule utilitarianism tests a rule permitting firms to hide unequal performance while fixes are underway. |
| Kant | The plan may have mixed intentions. Concealment is hard to universalize and denies patients and clinicians information needed for autonomous choice, treating them merely as means to funding. |
| Machiavelli egoism | Ethical if concealment preserves the company's power; but unethical in the longer term if exposure destroys influence. |
| Hobbes egoism | Cooperation and honest standards may better protect the firm's long-term self-interest and the stability of the health ecosystem. |
| Rand egoism | Ask whether leaders rationally and voluntarily pursue long-term interest rather than a short-term whim; coercing users or hiding material information complicates genuine choice. |
| Aristotle | Examine honesty, justice, courage, and prudence. The mean is not silence between overreaction and panic; proportionate action may require verified disclosure and rapid remediation. |
| Confucianism | Examine reciprocal duties among company, clinicians, patients, regulators, and employees. Profit cannot be the sole goal, and hierarchy should not erase duties to vulnerable groups. |

Theories can disagree without either analysis being defective. Each treats different considerations as morally central. Your job is to apply each charitably, expose its assumptions and limits, and then defend a conclusion. The table displays seven rows because the three egoist strands are separated, but they belong to one broader theoretical family.

### Understanding check 10

Which theory gives the most direct objection to sacrificing the minority group for aggregate benefit, and how could two other theories support the objection for different reasons?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

**Kantian ethics** gives the most direct objection because people must not be treated merely as means and need information for autonomous choice. **Aristotle** can object through justice, honesty, and compassion, while **Confucianism** can object through reciprocal duties and the requirement that business serve more than profit. Confucianism's weak rights doctrine is also a limitation. Utilitarianism may reach the same conclusion if long-term harms outweigh benefits, but it does not supply the same independent protection against sacrificing a minority.

</details>

# 12. How to score highly in the exams

This section converts the Week 1 assessment instructions and the lecturer's comments into an exam method. It is **not an official mark allocation**: the Weeks 1–2 materials do not provide one. The stated task is to identify ethical issues, analyze selected issues using at least two compelling and relevant theories, reach conclusions, and make practical recommendations. The examination is open-book but tests application rather than memorization. [W1, slides 18–21](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

## 12.1 Think in two scoring layers

### Layer 1: secure the core marks

Before attempting sophisticated criticism, make the basic analysis complete and accurate:

1. Identify a **specific act or omission** and the correct actor.
2. State the relevant theory's **course doctrine accurately**.
3. Apply **every element** of that doctrine to the facts.
4. Give arguments on both sides where the facts permit them.
5. Reach a clear, fact-sensitive conclusion.
6. If requested, give a practical recommendation linked to the ethical failure.

The lecturer repeatedly emphasized reproducing the doctrine and completing the basic analysis before adding extra commentary. A clever objection cannot compensate for skipping universalism under utilitarianism, respect for persons under Kant, voluntariness under Aristotle, or the precise strand of egoism.

### Layer 2: add top-band analysis

Once the core is present, add value through one or two **relevant** moves:

- expose an uncertain intention, consequence, stakeholder, or causal claim;
- label an assumption and show how the conclusion changes if it is false;
- present the strongest counterargument rather than an easy straw man;
- apply a theory-specific limitation to these facts;
- explain why two theories agree or disagree;
- distinguish short-term from long-term effects or self-interest;
- examine who bears risk and who receives benefit;
- make a recommendation that directly repairs the problem identified.

The lecturer specifically described relevant limitations as capable of lifting an answer beyond basic application. The important word is **relevant**: do not paste a generic criticism at the end of every paragraph.

## 12.2 Select theories by fit, not apparent difficulty

Using a more complicated theory does not automatically earn more credit. Select the theories that expose the most important features of the issue.

| Fact pattern contains... | Strong candidates | Why they fit |
|---|---|---|
| Many affected groups, risk, trade-offs, or competing policies | Act or rule utilitarianism | Consequences and alternatives are central. |
| Deception, coercion, consent, double standards, or hidden intentions | Kant | Intention, universalizability, and respect for persons are directly engaged. |
| Power seeking, strategic cooperation, voluntary choice, or long-term personal gain | The matching egoist strand | The facts identify a particular form of self-interest. |
| Habit, professional character, courage, loyalty, moderation, duress, or contextual judgment | Aristotle | Voluntariness, virtue, and the mean become material. |
| Family, hierarchy, favours, reciprocal duties, employer–employee relations, or profit versus public good | Confucianism | Relationships and role-based duties are central. |

Do not assume act and rule utilitarianism count as two separate theories merely to reach the required number: the slides describe them as two **versions** of utilitarianism. Unless the exam instructions or lecturer confirm otherwise, use both when the contrast matters but select another relevant ethical theory as well.

## 12.3 Budget time by issue, not by theory

First read the exact exam instructions because the required number of issues or theories may differ from the example in the slides. Then:

1. **Inventory the acts and omissions.** List actor + conduct in the margin.
2. **Rank the issues.** Prioritize those with rich facts and at least two clearly relevant theories.
3. **Allocate time.** Divide writing time among the required issues, preserving time for conclusions and recommendations.
4. **Stop on schedule.** A brilliant first issue cannot recover marks lost by leaving later required issues unanswered.

A useful open-book exam principle is `coverage first, depth second, polish third`. This is an exam strategy rather than a rule stated in the slides, but it follows from instructions requiring multiple issues and complete analyses.

## 12.4 Use a high-scoring paragraph architecture

For each theory applied to an issue, use this sequence:

```text
1. Doctrine: State the course rule accurately.
2. Element: Name the first component of the rule.
3. Fact: Tie a specific fact to that component.
4. Counterargument: Give the strongest opposing reading of the same facts.
5. Remaining elements: Repeat the element–fact analysis until the test is complete.
6. Limitation: Explain one fact-specific weakness or uncertainty.
7. Conclusion: Give a qualified but definite judgment under this theory.
```

This prevents two common failures: writing a theory summary with little application, and narrating facts without explaining their ethical significance.

### Weak versus strong application

**Weak:**

> Kant says people must be respected. The company hid the defect, so it was unethical.

**Stronger:**

> Kantian ethics requires good intention, a universalizable maxim, and respect for persons. The company's stated intention—preventing panic while engineers verified the defect—may be defensible; however, protecting its funding may indicate self-serving or deceptive intent. A maxim permitting companies to conceal material safety defects while seeking funding would undermine reliable disclosure if generally followed. Concealment also deprived users of information needed for autonomous choice, using them merely as a means to preserve investment. Although the intention is ambiguous, the failures of universalizability and respect make the concealment unethical under Kant.

The stronger answer earns its conclusion by completing the doctrine, using facts on both sides, exposing uncertainty, and explaining why each element passes or fails.

## 12.5 Handle missing facts without rewriting the hypothetical

Use one of these formulations:

```text
The facts do not state X. If X is true, then [...]. If X is false, then [...].

Assuming X, because [...], the likely result is [...]. This assumption is
material because without it [...].

It is reasonable to infer risk Y from fact Z, but not to assert that harm Y
actually occurred.
```

This is especially valuable when assessing:

- the scope and intensity of utilitarian consequences;
- Kantian intentions;
- whether Randian choice was uncoerced;
- Aristotelian voluntariness; or
- the existence and nature of a Confucian relationship.

An answer can reach different conclusions under different stated assumptions. That is not indecision; it is disciplined conditional reasoning.

## 12.6 Make criticisms earn their space

A strong limitation has three parts:

```text
Theory-specific problem → connection to a case fact → effect on confidence in the conclusion
```

Examples:

- **Utilitarianism:** The case gives no common scale for comparing minor convenience to severe injury, so the claimed net balance is uncertain.
- **Kant:** The facts do not reveal whether commercial self-interest counts as a bad, neutral, or mixed intention, weakening confidence in the first element.
- **Machiavelli:** A short-term display of power may trigger regulatory action and destroy long-term power, reversing the judgment.
- **Aristotle:** The theory does not precisely locate the courageous mean between silence and reckless disclosure, so the recommended action requires contestable judgment.
- **Confucianism:** Role-based hierarchy may explain duties but inadequately protect an employee whose individual rights are infringed.

Avoid: “This theory is subjective and therefore wrong.” Identify **what** is subjective, **why** it matters here, and whether it changes or merely qualifies the conclusion.

## 12.7 Recommendations should repair the diagnosed failure

Do not end with “the actor should behave more ethically.” Make the recommendation operational:

| Diagnosed problem | Better recommendation |
|---|---|
| Relevant stakeholders were excluded | Introduce a documented stakeholder and impact review before deployment. |
| Users lacked informed choice | Disclose the material risk plainly and obtain renewed consent. |
| A safety decision depended on one person's character | Add independent escalation and whistleblowing channels. |
| Profit became the sole objective | Add public-interest and employee-welfare criteria to approval decisions. |
| A recurring case invites opportunistic exceptions | Establish a generally applicable rule with a justified emergency exception. |

The causal link matters: state how the proposal reduces harm, respects autonomy, cultivates virtue, fulfils reciprocal duties, or otherwise responds to your chosen theory.

## 12.8 Open-book preparation that actually saves time

Do not bring a long set of notes that you have never navigated. Build a compact lookup system:

- one page comparing all theories;
- one exact doctrine box per theory or strand;
- numbered application elements beneath each doctrine;
- two or three fact triggers showing when the theory fits;
- one serious limitation and one worked mini-example;
- tabs or a contents page for rapid retrieval.

The Week 1 slides explicitly recommend making your own notes so that you know their structure before the open-book examination. [W1, slides 35–39](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

## 12.9 Final five-minute scoring audit

Before submitting, ask:

- Did I satisfy the exact number of issues and theories requested?
- Is each issue framed as actor + precise act or omission?
- Did I state each doctrine accurately and apply every element?
- Did I distinguish facts, inferences, and assumptions?
- Did I analyze rather than merely describe the theory?
- Did I give a real counterargument and a relevant limitation?
- Is each conclusion clear and supported?
- Is every recommendation practical and tied to the analysis?
- Have I avoided treating legality, company policy, and ethics as interchangeable?

# 13. Exam-ready application template

The hypothetical assessment format asks you to identify issues, apply at least two compelling theories to each selected issue, give balanced analysis, conclude, and recommend improvements. [W1, slides 19–21](<../../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

Use this paragraph structure:

```text
Issue
Whether [actor]'s act of [precise conduct] was ethical.

Doctrine
State the course formulation accurately.

Application
Work through every element using only the facts. Identify stakeholders,
uncertainties, and expressly labelled assumptions. Consider the strongest
argument on both sides.

Limitation
Explain one theory-specific weakness that matters to these facts.

Conclusion
Give a qualified but definite judgment.

Recommendation
Propose a realistic change tied to the failure your analysis identified.
```

### Fast theory checklists

**Utilitarianism**

- Precise act or rule?
- Everyone affected?
- Pleasure and pain, including risk and long-term effects?
- All feasible alternatives compared?
- Greatest net balance?
- Measurement, subjectivity, scope, or distribution problem?

**Kant**

- Good intention?
- Maxim stated fairly?
- Universalizable without contradiction or special pleading?
- Human autonomy and dignity respected?
- Duty conflict or rationality problem?

**Egoism**

- Which strand?
- What exactly counts as the actor's self-interest here?
- Short-term versus long-term power or welfare?
- Cooperation, rationality, voluntariness, or coercion?
- Strand-specific criticism?

**Aristotle**

- Voluntary act?
- Relevant virtue or vice?
- Deficiency, contextual mean, and excess?
- Rational basis for locating the mean?
- Effect on character and human flourishing?

**Confucianism**

- Which listed relationship, or what explicit analogy?
- Duties on both sides?
- Reciprocity, hierarchy, and family priority?
- Virtue/public good versus profit alone?
- Rights, dignity, or power criticism?

# 14. What matters most

1. The theories disagree because they answer different moral questions: outcomes, duty, self-interest, character, and relationships.
2. The most important Week 1 distinction is **act versus rule utilitarianism**: evaluate the particular act versus a generally accepted utility-maximizing rule.
3. The most important Week 2 distinction is what each theory treats as morally central. Do not reduce Kant to “rules,” egoism to “selfishness,” Aristotle to “be moderate,” or Confucianism to “obey hierarchy.”
4. In every hypothetical, separate facts from assumptions and complete the theory's full test before concluding.

## Final retrieval practice

Without looking back, write one sentence for each theory beginning:

- “Utilitarianism judges...”
- “Kantian ethics judges...”
- “Machiavelli, Hobbes, and Rand differ because...”
- “Aristotle judges...”
- “Confucianism judges...”

Then apply any two to a new case from study, work, family life, or public policy. If the two theories agree, check whether they agree **for different reasons**. That distinction is often where the best analysis begins.
