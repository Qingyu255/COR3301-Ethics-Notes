# Navigating Morality and Ethics

## The central idea

Ethical judgment is not about finding a rule that produces an automatic answer. It is about making your reasoning visible:

```text
What happened?
      ↓
Who is affected?
      ↓
Which values and duties matter?
      ↓
What realistic alternatives exist?
      ↓
Which conclusion has the strongest reasons?
```

This guide provides a practical method for personal decisions, professional dilemmas, and course case studies. For the complete course theories, see the [Weeks 1–5 overview](<../course-overview-weeks-01-05.md>).

---

## 1. Morality and ethics

The terms often overlap, but this distinction is useful:

- **Morality** refers to beliefs, norms, and practices about right and wrong. These may come from family, culture, religion, experience, or personal conviction.
- **Ethics** is the reasoned examination of those beliefs. It asks why a choice is right, whether the principle is consistent, whose interests count, and how objections should be answered.

Morality might produce the reaction, “Lying is wrong.” Ethical reasoning asks:

- Why is lying normally wrong?
- Does the rule apply when a lie protects someone from unjust violence?
- How are trust, autonomy, intention, and consequences affected?
- What alternative avoids both deception and harm?

Ethical reflection does not require abandoning moral convictions. It tests and clarifies them.

## 2. Describe before judging

Before calling something ethical or unethical, separate four categories:

| Category | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Fact | supported by available evidence | “The advertisement said the product was running low.” |
| Inference | a conclusion reasonably drawn from facts | “The message probably increased urgency.” |
| Assumption | something accepted temporarily without sufficient proof | “Assume the company knew the stock was plentiful.” |
| Normative judgment | a claim about what ought to happen | “The company should remove the message.” |

A factual description does not produce an ethical conclusion by itself. It needs a value premise:

```text
Fact:       The company knowingly displayed false stock information.
Principle:  Businesses ought not manipulate consumer choices using false facts.
Conclusion: The company ought not display the message.
```

Emotions also matter without settling the answer. Anger may reveal perceived injustice; guilt may reveal responsibility; discomfort may reveal a conflict of values. Treat the emotion as a signal to investigate, not as proof.

---

## 3. Frame the precise ethical issue

Avoid questions that are too broad:

> Is this company ethical?

Use this structure instead:

> Whether **[specific actor]** acted ethically by **[specific act or omission]** in **[relevant circumstances]**.

For example, an AI case may contain several different issues:

- whether developers designed adequate safeguards;
- whether a company released the system after discovering a risk;
- whether a user relied on an unverified answer;
- whether a professional presented that answer as fact; and
- whether a regulator imposed a proportionate rule.

Different actors can receive different judgments.

---

## 4. Map stakeholders, power, and vulnerability

Ask who:

- receives the benefits;
- bears the harms or risks;
- has meaningful choice or consent;
- possesses more information or authority;
- can prevent or repair the harm;
- is absent from the decision;
- will be affected in the future; and
- is especially vulnerable because of age, dependence, illness, poverty, coercion, or limited knowledge.

A particularly important warning sign is:

> One group receives most of the benefit while another group involuntarily carries most of the risk.

Greater power does not make an actor automatically wrong, but it normally creates stronger responsibilities of explanation, care, and restraint.

## 5. Use several ethical lenses

### Consequences: utilitarianism

Ask which realistic option produces the greatest overall balance of welfare over harm for everyone affected.

Consider:

- probability;
- intensity;
- duration;
- number of people affected;
- reversibility;
- distribution of benefits and burdens; and
- short- and long-term effects.

Do not assume that a small benefit to many automatically outweighs severe harm to a few.

### Intention, consistency, and dignity: Kant

Ask:

1. Is the intention good or morally defensible?
2. Could the actor rationally will the maxim as a universal rule?
3. Does the act respect people as ends rather than merely using them as instruments?

All three requirements matter. Good intentions alone do not excuse deception or disrespect.

### Self-interest: ethical egoism

Specify which version you mean:

- **Machiavelli:** Does the act acquire or preserve power?
- **Hobbes:** Is cooperation in the actor's interest?
- **Rand:** Does the act rationally and voluntarily advance long-term flourishing?

Immediate desire is not necessarily rational long-term self-interest.

### Character: Aristotle

Ask whether the act is voluntary, which virtue is relevant, and whether the act expresses the context-sensitive mean between deficiency and excess.

```text
cowardice ← courage → rashness
deception ← honesty → needlessly cruel bluntness
neglect ← compassionate care → controlling overprotection
```

The mean is not an arithmetic midpoint. It is the response that practical wisdom judges appropriate to the circumstances.

### Relationships and reciprocal duties: Confucianism

Ask:

- What relationship exists?
- What does each side owe the other?
- Does authority fulfil its responsibility of humane care?
- Does the conduct support virtue, trust, and public good?

For modern relationships, explain any analogy rather than pretending it is one of the traditional relationships. Duties are reciprocal: hierarchy does not justify unconditional obedience.

### Justice as a cross-cutting question

Whichever theory you use, also ask:

- Are similar cases treated consistently?
- Are benefits and burdens distributed fairly?
- Is anyone excluded from an opportunity without a relevant reason?
- Does the decision reinforce an unfair power structure?

## 6. Recognize serious warning signs

The following features demand especially strong justification:

- coercion or absence of meaningful consent;
- deception or material omission;
- exploitation of vulnerability;
- severe or irreversible harm;
- using someone merely as a tool;
- abuse of professional or institutional authority;
- transferring risk to people who receive little benefit;
- concealing a conflict of interest; and
- creating an exception you would reject if someone else used it.

These features do not replace careful analysis, but they help identify what needs the closest attention.

---

## 7. Compare realistic alternatives

Do not assume that the only choices are “perform the act” and “do nothing.” Consider whether you could:

- disclose information and obtain meaningful consent;
- preserve the benefit through a less harmful method;
- delay until essential facts are verified;
- consult an independent person;
- choose a reversible option under uncertainty;
- introduce safeguards or human review;
- compensate affected parties;
- narrow a policy rather than impose a total ban; or
- acknowledge and repair an earlier wrong.

An apparently insoluble dilemma often becomes easier when a third option changes how the benefits and burdens are distributed.

---

## 8. Converge without counting theory votes

If four theories oppose an act and one supports it, the answer is not automatically “four against one.” Compare the reasons:

1. **Relevance:** Which theory directly addresses the central issue?
2. **Completeness:** Was the full doctrine applied?
3. **Evidence:** How reliable are the decisive facts?
4. **Seriousness:** Are coercion, dignity, grave harm, or irreversible loss involved?
5. **Alternatives:** Could the benefit be preserved more safely?
6. **Uncertainty:** What missing fact could reverse the conclusion?

Possible conclusions include:

| Classification | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ethical outright | decisive support and no serious unresolved objection |
| Ethical to a large extent | strong support with limited concerns |
| Ethical to a small extent | some justification, but major reservations remain |
| Neither clearly ethical nor unethical | known reasons remain genuinely balanced |
| Indeterminate | essential evidence is missing |
| Unethical to a small extent | a limited failure with substantial mitigation |
| Unethical to a large extent | serious failures with limited mitigation |
| Unethical outright | a grave and unjustifiable violation |

“It depends” is not a conclusion until you identify exactly what it depends on.

## 9. Worked example: a friend asks you to conceal cheating

### The issue

> Whether you should lie to conceal a friend's academic cheating.

### Applying the lenses

| Lens | Analysis |
|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | The lie may protect the friend temporarily, but harms honest students, institutional trust, and possibly the friend's long-term development. |
| Kant | A universal permission to lie whenever friends face consequences would undermine trust. The person receiving the lie is manipulated. |
| Machiavelli | Concealment might preserve influence in the friendship, but discovery could destroy credibility and power. |
| Hobbes | Cooperation under fair academic rules protects the interests of students; secret cheating undermines that arrangement. |
| Rand | Lying may create anxiety and reputational risk rather than rational long-term flourishing. |
| Aristotle | Loyalty matters, but loyalty without honesty becomes enabling. Courage may require a difficult conversation. |
| Confucianism | Friendship creates duties of care and sincerity. Care need not mean concealing harmful conduct. |

### A better alternative

Refuse to lie, but help the friend take responsibility, explain mitigating circumstances honestly, and seek a proportionate resolution. This preserves care without participating in deception.

### Qualification

If disclosure would expose the friend to serious unjust violence rather than proportionate accountability, the ethical balance changes. The relevant facts and available protective alternatives would need to be reassessed.

## 10. Five questions for everyday decisions

Before acting, ask:

1. Would I accept this if our positions were reversed?
2. What would happen if everyone used the same justification?
3. Am I giving affected people enough information and choice?
4. Who receives the benefit, and who carries the risk?
5. Could I explain this decision honestly to the people affected?

For high-stakes decisions, add:

- What am I assuming?
- What is the most serious plausible harm?
- Is the choice reversible?
- Who should I consult?
- What will I do if I am wrong?

---

## 11. Moral disagreement

When another person disagrees:

1. Restate their position in a form they recognize.
2. Identify whether the disagreement concerns facts, values, or both.
3. Find shared principles before arguing about conclusions.
4. Ask what evidence would change either person's view.
5. Avoid treating confidence, social approval, or legal permission as proof of ethical correctness.
6. If a decision must be made, choose the most justifiable and reversible option while protecting those facing the greatest risk.

Respectful disagreement does not mean every position is equally well supported. It means criticizing the reasons accurately rather than dismissing the person.

---

## 12. Ethical mistakes and repair

Ethical navigation includes what happens after a mistake. A meaningful response normally involves:

1. stopping the harmful conduct;
2. acknowledging the specific wrong without minimizing it;
3. listening to affected people;
4. repairing or compensating harm where possible;
5. explaining how the failure occurred; and
6. changing the conditions that allowed it to recur.

An apology focused only on intention—“I did not mean to hurt anyone”—is incomplete if it ignores foreseeable impact and prevention.

---

## Final synthesis

Ethical maturity is not possessing immediate certainty. It is the ability to:

- notice morally relevant facts;
- distinguish evidence from assumption;
- take other people's perspectives seriously;
- test convenient justifications;
- choose proportionate and defensible action;
- remain open to correction; and
- repair harm when necessary.

The goal is not moral perfection. It is increasingly honest, consistent, humane, and responsible judgment.
