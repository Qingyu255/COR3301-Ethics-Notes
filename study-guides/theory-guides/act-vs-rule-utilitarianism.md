# Act versus Rule Utilitarianism

## The simplest difference

Both versions of utilitarianism aim to maximize overall welfare for everyone affected. They differ in **what is evaluated first**.

```text
ACT UTILITARIANISM
Evaluate this particular act in these particular circumstances.

RULE UTILITARIANISM
First identify the generally accepted rule that would maximize welfare.
Then ask whether the particular act follows that rule.
```

Neither version is automatically superior. The difference is between deciding case by case and deciding through welfare-promoting general rules.

This guide follows the course treatment in the [Weeks 1–2 study guide](<../week-01-02/ethics_weeks_1_2_study_guide.md>) and [Weeks 1–5 overview](<../course-overview-weeks-01-05.md>).

## 1. What both versions share

Both are utilitarian because both judge ultimately by consequences for overall welfare.

Both require you to:

1. count **everyone affected**, not only the actor;
2. consider both pleasure or benefit and pain or harm;
3. compare realistic alternatives;
4. consider short- and long-term effects;
5. examine probability, intensity, duration, scale, and distribution; and
6. choose the option that produces the greatest net balance of welfare.

The difference is the level at which that calculation occurs.

---

## 2. Act utilitarianism

### Central question

> Among the available actions in this particular situation, which one is expected to produce the greatest net welfare for everyone affected?

### Application steps

1. Define the precise act.
2. Identify realistic alternatives.
3. Identify every materially affected stakeholder.
4. Estimate the benefits and harms of each alternative.
5. Compare likelihood, intensity, duration, reversibility, and distribution.
6. Select the act with the greatest expected net welfare.

### Example: running a red light

Suppose Nick approaches a red light late at night. No other vehicle or pedestrian appears to be nearby.

Act utilitarianism asks about **this crossing now**:

- Benefit: Nick reaches his destination slightly sooner.
- Harms: collision risk, risk from unseen road users, anxiety, and possible effects on other drivers.
- Alternative: wait briefly for the light to change.

If the small time saving does not outweigh the remaining safety risk, running the light is unethical. In a genuine emergency where delay risks a life and the intersection is carefully checked, the calculation could change.

### Strength

Act utilitarianism is sensitive to exceptional facts. It can recognize that an action normally harmful may create the best outcome in a genuine emergency.

### Limitation

It may justify troubling exceptions whenever breaking an ordinary rule appears to increase welfare. It also demands difficult calculations for every decision.

---

## 3. Rule utilitarianism

### Central question

> Which generally accepted rule for comparable situations would produce the greatest net welfare, and does this act conform to that rule?

### Application steps

1. Define the type of recurring situation.
2. Formulate two or more plausible general rules.
3. Imagine each rule being generally accepted and followed.
4. Compare their overall effects on everyone.
5. Select the rule with the greatest net welfare.
6. Ask whether the particular act follows that rule.

### Example: running a red light

Possible candidate rules include:

- Rule A: “Drivers may run red lights whenever they personally believe the road is clear.”
- Rule B: “Drivers must stop at red lights, except for carefully defined emergency vehicles or emergency conditions.”

If Rule A were generally accepted, drivers would make self-serving mistakes, road behaviour would become unpredictable, and collisions would likely increase. Rule B creates a stable expectation while allowing narrowly defined emergencies.

If Rule B produces greater welfare, Nick's ordinary decision to run the light is unethical because it violates the selected rule.

### Strength

Rule utilitarianism emphasizes predictability, trust, equal treatment, and the long-term value of stable practices.

### Limitation

It can become rigid when an unusual case was not anticipated. It can also be manipulated by writing a rule so narrowly that it merely disguises a one-off exception.

## 4. The same case through both versions

### Case: lying to protect a friend from embarrassment

Your friend made a minor but blameworthy mistake and asks you to lie about it.

#### Act utilitarianism

Ask whether **this lie now** produces more welfare than honesty or another response.

- The lie may spare immediate embarrassment.
- It may prevent learning and accountability.
- It may harm someone blamed instead.
- Discovery may damage several relationships.
- A truthful but compassionate response may preserve more welfare.

The answer depends on the concrete consequences of this act and its alternatives.

#### Rule utilitarianism

Compare generally accepted rules:

- “People may lie whenever honesty would embarrass a friend.”
- “People should be honest about relevant wrongdoing while responding compassionately and protecting privacy where possible.”

The first rule would weaken trust and encourage concealment. The second is more likely to preserve trust while limiting unnecessary humiliation. Therefore, the lie is unethical if it violates the welfare-maximizing rule.

---

## 5. A comparison that is easier to remember

| Feature | Act utilitarianism | Rule utilitarianism |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate object of evaluation | the particular act | a generally accepted rule, followed by the act's conformity |
| Main question | “What produces the best outcome here?” | “What general rule produces the best outcomes, and does this act follow it?” |
| Level of reasoning | case by case | recurring practice or institution |
| Main advantage | adapts to exceptional circumstances | supports stability, trust, and predictable expectations |
| Main danger | may excuse harmful one-off exceptions | may become rigid or hide exceptions through narrow wording |
| Final basis | consequences | consequences of general acceptance |

Rule utilitarianism does **not** ignore consequences. Consequences justify the rule.

---

## 6. How to formulate a proper rule

A useful rule should be:

- **general:** it applies to everyone in comparable circumstances;
- **clear:** people can understand what conduct it permits;
- **practical:** it can guide recurring decisions;
- **public:** its value does not depend on secret exceptions; and
- **compared with alternatives:** it is selected because its general acceptance produces the greatest welfare.

### Bad rule

> “Students may cheat when Qingyu personally needs a higher grade.”

This is not a meaningful general rule. It is a personalized exception.

### Better candidate rules

- “Students may use unauthorized assistance whenever they fear receiving a low grade.”
- “Students must complete assessed work without unauthorized assistance.”
- “Students must complete assessed work honestly, with authorized accommodations for documented needs.”

Compare the general consequences of each rule for learning, fairness, trust, stress, and accessibility. Then test the act against the best rule.

---

## 7. Can rule utilitarianism contain exceptions?

Yes, but an exception must be part of a defensible general rule rather than invented after the fact to benefit one person.

For example:

> “People should not enter restricted areas, except when entry is reasonably necessary to prevent imminent serious harm.”

This is different from:

> “People should not enter restricted areas, except when I personally want to.”

To test an exception, ask:

1. Can it be defined using morally relevant facts?
2. Would it apply to everyone in comparable circumstances?
3. Would general acceptance of the exception improve welfare?
4. Can people apply it without making the main rule ineffective?

If exceptions multiply until the rule becomes “do whatever seems best in each case,” rule utilitarianism begins to collapse into act utilitarianism.

## 8. Worked business example: artificial scarcity

A retailer displays “Only two left—buy now!” even though its warehouse contains thousands of units.

### Act utilitarian analysis

Consider this particular message:

- Benefits: increased sales, excitement, possible convenience for buyers who wanted the item.
- Harms: pressured purchases, regret, wasted money, reduced trust, complaints, and reputational or regulatory consequences.
- Alternative: display accurate stock information or a truthful promotion deadline.

If consumer and social harms exceed the commercial benefits, the act is unethical.

### Rule utilitarian analysis

Compare:

- “Retailers may fabricate scarcity when doing so increases sales.”
- “Retailers must provide materially accurate scarcity information.”

General acceptance of fabricated scarcity would make stock messages unreliable and increase manipulation. An accuracy rule would likely preserve consumer trust and improve long-term market welfare. The false message violates that rule and is therefore unethical.

### Why both reach the same answer differently

- Act utilitarianism focuses on the effects of this false message.
- Rule utilitarianism focuses on the effects of allowing this kind of message as a general practice.

Agreement in conclusion does not erase the difference in reasoning.

## 9. When the two versions may disagree

Suppose lying in one unusual situation seems certain to prevent harm and has almost no risk of discovery.

- **Act utilitarianism** may permit the lie because its particular consequences are beneficial.
- **Rule utilitarianism** may reject it if a general permission to lie in similar cases would weaken trust and be widely abused.

The disagreement concerns whether welfare is best protected through flexible case-by-case judgment or stable general expectations.

However, do not manufacture disagreement. Both versions may reach the same conclusion, and a carefully formulated rule may include genuine emergency exceptions.

## 10. Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: “Act utilitarianism means doing whatever I want”

No. It requires maximizing welfare for **everyone affected**, not the actor alone.

### Mistake 2: “Rule utilitarianism means obeying existing law”

No. Existing laws may be harmful. The selected rule is the rule whose general acceptance produces the greatest welfare.

### Mistake 3: evaluating one convenient rule

You must compare plausible alternative rules. A rule does not win merely because it sounds sensible.

### Mistake 4: writing a personalized rule

“I may cheat when I need to” is a disguised exception, not an appropriately general rule.

### Mistake 5: calculating only immediate consequences

Include long-term trust, habits, incentives, precedent, enforcement, and indirect effects.

### Mistake 6: treating headcount as the whole calculation

The intensity and duration of harm matter. Mild amusement for many does not automatically outweigh grave harm to a few.

### Mistake 7: assuming rule utilitarianism is more advanced

Neither version is inherently better or more impressive. Use the required version, or explain why the contrast matters.

## 12. Quick decision guide

```text
Are you evaluating this one act in its exact circumstances?
                         ↓
               Use act utilitarianism

Are you evaluating a recurring practice, policy, or rule?
                         ↓
               Use rule utilitarianism

Does the case involve a possible one-off exception to a useful rule?
                         ↓
               Apply both and explain the tension
```

## 13. Self-check

### Questions

1. Why is rule utilitarianism still consequentialist?
2. What is wrong with the rule “People may lie whenever lying benefits them personally”?
3. Can a rule contain exceptions without becoming act utilitarianism?
4. Why might act and rule utilitarianism disagree about a harmless-looking lie?
5. Does the fact that an action is illegal prove it is wrong under rule utilitarianism?

### Answers

1. The rule is selected because the consequences of its general acceptance maximize welfare.
2. It privileges the actor, does not protect everyone's welfare, and is likely to destroy trust if generally accepted.
3. Yes. The exception must be general, relevant, welfare-promoting, and usable without collapsing the rule.
4. The particular lie may appear beneficial, while general permission for comparable lies may damage trust and invite abuse.
5. No. The law and the welfare-maximizing rule can differ; the legal rule itself must be ethically evaluated.

---

## Final memory aid

> **Act utilitarianism:** compare the consequences of the available acts in this case.

> **Rule utilitarianism:** compare the consequences of generally accepted rules, choose the best rule, and test the act against it.

Both aim at the same destination—maximum overall welfare—but take different routes to reach it.
