## 2. A reusable framework for advertising cases

### Step 1: Identify the exact act

Separate related acts:

- creating the advertisement;
- approving and publishing it;
- targeting a particular audience;
- making a literal or implied claim;
- keeping the campaign online after concerns arise;
- apologizing, defending, editing, or withdrawing it.

Different actors may be responsible: the company, creative agency, executives, platform, consultant, influencer, or consumer.

### Step 2: Identify the advertisement's overall impression

Ask:

- What does the advertisement literally say?
- What does it imply through images, wordplay, placement, or omitted information?
- What would a reasonable audience probably understand?
- Could different cultural or social contexts change that meaning?
- Is a qualification prominent enough to correct the main impression?

A technically defensible sentence can still be misleading when the overall presentation creates a false impression.

### Step 3: Separate intention from impact

```text
Intention: What was the advertiser trying to achieve?
Impact:    What effects did the advertisement actually or foreseeably produce?
```

- Kant directly examines intention, universalizability, and respect.
- Utilitarianism directly examines consequences.
- Aristotle asks what character the conduct expresses.
- Egoism asks how the conduct serves the actor's self-interest.

Good intention does not erase harmful impact, and harmful impact does not prove a malicious intention. Analyze both.

### Step 4: Map the stakeholders

Possible stakeholders include:

- consumers and vulnerable audiences;
- people represented or stereotyped;
- employees and models;
- competitors;
- regulators;
- shareholders and business partners;
- communities affected by misinformation; and
- the wider public's trust in advertising.

### Step 5: Compare realistic alternatives

Do not compare the advertisement with silence alone. Alternatives might include accurate qualifications, less manipulative design, broader representation, independent review, a clearer correction, or a different campaign concept.

## 3. Student presentation: deceptive TikTok livestream selling

The student presentation examines livestream sellers who use tactics such as false scarcity, repeated countdowns, exaggerated product claims, emotional stories, and suppression of complaints to produce purchases that viewers might not otherwise make. The presentation concludes that these deceptive practices are unethical under all the theories it applies, although the reasoning and strength of each conclusion differ. (Week 4 transcript, approximately 00:00–00:39.)

### Utilitarianism

- **Act utilitarianism:** sellers, platforms, and entertained viewers may gain money or pleasure, but deceived consumers may lose money, receive inferior products, and suffer continuing distrust. The result depends on the number of people affected and the intensity and duration of their experiences, not on headcount alone.
- **Rule utilitarianism:** compare general rules such as “livestream sellers may fabricate scarcity to increase sales” and “sellers must not make false material representations.” A deception-permitting rule would likely destroy trust and reduce welfare when generally accepted.

During the Q&A, the lecturer highlighted two limitations of utility calculations: intensity and duration are different measures that cannot be converted objectively, and large amounts of minor entertainment could theoretically outweigh severe harm to a small minority. A good answer must confront rather than hide these implications. (Transcript, approximately 00:41–00:47.)

### Kantian ethics

Deceptive tactics can fail all three course requirements:

1. an intention to manipulate for profit is not a good intention;
2. universal deception would destroy the trust on which the practice depends; and
3. false or irrelevant information interferes with rational consumer choice, treating buyers as instruments of revenue.

A seller's intention may instead be neutral or even supportive of family, but that does not repair failures of universalizability or respect. The lecturer emphasized that Kantian conclusions can also be expressed by degree: if only an ambiguous intention fails while universalizability and respect succeed, the act may be unethical only to a small extent. If respect fails clearly, the judgment is much stronger. (Transcript, approximately 00:58–01:01.)

### Ethical egoism

- **Machiavelli:** urgency and attention may increase short-term consumer control, market position, and audience influence. Backlash, sanctions, and lost trust may reduce long-term power.
- **Hobbes:** honest seller–buyer cooperation can generate repeat business and stable mutual benefit; persistent deception undermines that cooperation.
- **Rand:** immediate revenue may look self-interested, but reputational, platform, and legal risks may make it irrational over the long term.

The case demonstrates why an egoist answer must name both the actor and the time horizon.

### Aristotle

Repeated false scarcity is likely voluntary. Claiming not to have checked the truth may amount to willful blindness, not excusable ignorance. The presentation identifies:

| Virtue | Deficiency | Context-sensitive mean | Excess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honesty | deceptive claims for profit | truthful disclosure of facts material to purchase | irrelevant or cruel bluntness |
| Compassion | treating consumers only as revenue | genuine concern while preserving informed choice | overprotection that removes all consumer choice |

“Puffery” is a weak defence when viewers reasonably understand a fabricated statement as fact. The mean is not a moderate amount of deception.

### Confucianism

The presentation analogizes seller–buyer to ruler–subject because the seller has influence, but openly acknowledges that this is a weak fit: the transaction does not match a traditional relationship neatly. Deception conflicts with sincerity, benevolence, reciprocity, and public trust. A strong answer should state both the possible analogy and its limitation rather than forcing the theory.

### What the presentation teaches

- Apply every limb of the chosen doctrine before adding criticism.
- Compare short- and long-term consequences.
- Distinguish harmless exaggeration from factual claims that affect choice.
- Analyze the same practice from the perspective of sellers, platforms, consumers, and vulnerable viewers.
- A conclusion can have a degree; “unethical” need not mean “equally unethical in every respect.”

## 4. Case 1: Was Swatch being racist?

The slides show an advertisement in which a model pulls at the corners of his eyes, a gesture widely criticized as a racist depiction of East Asian features. They also state that China, Hong Kong, and Macau account for approximately 27% of Swatch's revenue. Swatch removed the materials, apologized for distress or misunderstanding, and reaffirmed its commitment to inclusivity. [W4, slides 4-8](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

### Separate the issues

1. Was publishing the advertisement ethical?
2. Was Swatch's response ethical?
3. Did the apology accept responsibility, or frame the problem mainly as audience misunderstanding?

### Applying the theories

**Utilitarianism**

- Possible benefit: attention, product sales, creative expression.
- Possible harms: humiliation, normalization of racial stereotypes, distress, employee or consumer alienation, reputational loss, and reduced trust.
- The economic importance of the affected markets is relevant to consequences, but harm is not important only when victims are profitable customers.

The limited commercial benefits are unlikely to outweigh the foreseeable social and reputational harms if the gesture is reasonably understood as racist.

**Kantian ethics**

- Intention is uncertain; do not automatically infer deliberate racism.
- A maxim permitting companies to use demeaning racial imagery whenever it attracts attention is difficult to universalize.
- Reducing a person's racial identity to a stereotype may fail to respect persons as ends.

Even without proof of malicious intention, failures of universalizability or respect can make the advertisement unethical under Kant.

**Egoism**

- The campaign may have sought profit or attention.
- Under Machiavelli, backlash and lost influence can turn a short-term attention strategy into a loss of power.
- Under Rand, a foreseeable reputational crisis may not be rational long-term self-interest.

**Aristotle**

Relevant virtues include respectfulness, sensitivity, practical wisdom, and honesty. The advertisement may show a deficiency of cultural judgment rather than the virtuous mean.

### Evaluating the response

Removing the advertisement reduces further harm and the apology acknowledges concern. However, apologizing only for "distress or misunderstanding" can appear to avoid naming the wrong. A stronger response would explain how approval failed, accept proportionate responsibility, and describe safeguards against recurrence.

## 5. Cases 2 and 3: American Eagle, Gap, and competition

### American Eagle

The campaign used the phrase "Sydney Sweeney Has Great Jeans," creating wordplay between "jeans" and "genes." The slides then show American Eagle's response that the campaign was always about jeans, followed by a critical image claiming that "genes" had appeared in the advertisement and was later crossed out. [W4, slides 9-13](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

The ethical issue is not settled by either "it was only wordplay" or "everyone understood the same hidden message." Ask:

- What meanings were reasonably foreseeable?
- Did the campaign exploit ambiguity deliberately?
- Which audiences were affected, and how seriously?
- Did the response engage the criticism or merely restate the intended meaning?

**Kant:** If ambiguity was used to gain attention while denying the implication, the campaign may undermine informed audience interpretation. Intention remains an evidential question.

**Utilitarianism:** Compare entertainment and sales against polarization, stereotype reinforcement, offence, and reputational effects. The intensity and reach of each effect matter more than the number of social-media comments.

**Aristotle:** Examine honesty, sensitivity, courage, and practical wisdom. A virtuous response would neither dismiss reasonable concern nor confess to an intention unsupported by facts.

### Gap and "healthy competition"

The slides present Gap's denim advertisement and the physical placement of the Gap and Sydney Sweeney advertisements near each other, then ask whether business competition is ethical. [W4, slides 14-17](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

Competition is not automatically ethical or unethical.

| Theory | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | Does competition improve quality, choice, creativity, or price? Does it also create waste, deception, hostility, or harmful pressure? Who counts as "everyone"? |
| Kant | Is the competitor treated as a legitimate rival or merely manipulated and humiliated? Could the competitive practice be universalized? |
| Machiavelli | Did the campaign gain market power, attention, or influence? Did backlash weaken that power? |
| Hobbes | Can fair rules create beneficial competition by enforcing cooperation with a shared market framework? |
| Aristotle | Does the campaign express excellence and confidence, or envy, dishonesty, and excess? |

The most defensible conclusion is that fair competition can be ethical when it improves consumer welfare and respects common rules. Deceptive, exclusionary, or degrading competition is harder to justify.

## 6. Case 4: Ragebaiting

The slides define **ragebaiting** as online content designed to produce rage or anger, generally to increase engagement, reach, or support. [W4, slides 18-20](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

### Why it is ethically difficult

Rage can draw attention to real injustice, but it can also be manufactured through distortion, selective framing, or deliberate provocation. Therefore, distinguish:

- truthful content that reasonably produces anger;
- exaggerated content designed to intensify anger; and
- false or decontextualized content created mainly for engagement.

### Applying the theories

**Kant**

- What is the intention: informing, mobilizing, entertaining, or manipulating?
- Could a rule permitting deliberate emotional manipulation for engagement be universalized?
- Does the content respect the audience's rational agency, or use anger as a means to clicks?

**Machiavelli**

Ragebait can generate power through reach and mobilization. But loss of trust, platform penalties, and escalating hostility may undermine long-term power.

**Hobbes**

If ragebait weakens trust and common rules, it may promote the insecurity or disorder that cooperation is meant to avoid.

**Rand**

Short-term engagement is not automatically rational self-interest. Reputational damage, dependency on outrage, and platform sanctions may make the strategy self-defeating.

**Aristotle**

Relevant virtues include honesty, temperance, justice, courage, and practical wisdom. The mean is not "a moderate amount of deception." It is proportionate emotional communication that truthfully serves a defensible purpose.

## 7. Case 5: Can air-conditioning "save the Earth"?

The slides present a PRISM+ advertisement describing use of its air-conditioners as the "best tip" to "save the Earth." They also state an advertising principle that advertisements should not mislead through inaccuracy, ambiguity, exaggeration, omission, or misrepresentation. PRISM+ defended the campaign as playful, tongue-in-cheek, and satirical. [W4, slides 21-24](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

### The central distinction

```text
Obvious satire that a reasonable audience will not take literally
                              versus
Environmental exaggeration that can influence purchasing decisions
```

Calling something humour does not automatically remove responsibility. Ask whether the satire is clear to the expected audience and whether the environmental message is likely to influence attitudes toward the product.

**Kant:** Even with a playful intention, misleading environmental claims may fail universalizability and respect for consumers' autonomous choices.

**Utilitarianism:** Compare amusement and useful efficiency information with confusion, increased consumption, environmental impact, and weakened trust in genuine sustainability claims.

**Egoism:** The advertiser, agency, consumers, and regulator have different interests. A short-term sales benefit can be outweighed by regulatory and reputational costs.

**Aristotle:** The mean lies between dull literalism and reckless exaggeration, but it must be justified through honesty and practical wisdom. The mean can shift as audiences become more aware of greenwashing.

## 8. Case 6: iTea's "zero" claim

The advertisement appeared to claim that certain drinks were zero-sugar and zero-calorie. The slides say iTea was supposedly referring to a new sugar substitute containing zero calories, while consumers were confused by the overall claim. [W4, slides 25-27](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

### Why literal truth may be insufficient

If only the substitute was zero-calorie but the completed drink was not, the advertisement's overall impression could be misleading even if one component claim was true.

**Kant:** A maxim allowing businesses to rely on hidden qualifications while encouraging a broader false impression cannot easily be universalized. Confusing consumers may treat them as means to sales.

**Egoism:** Regulatory action by the Singapore Food Agency or Health Promotion Board could reduce the company's power and make the campaign contrary to long-term self-interest.

**Utilitarianism:** Rules against misleading food advertisements may increase welfare by protecting health decisions, trust, and fair competition, although enforcement costs and overly rigid rules should also be considered.

The best practical response is a prominent statement of what is zero-calorie: the substitute, the base drink, or the finished customized drink.

## 9. Case 7: McKinsey, HBO, and ethical consulting

The slides state that McKinsey charged Warner Bros. for advice concerning its merger with Discovery, repeated streaming-service renaming, and a later split. They cite figures of $55 million, $37 million, and $63 million respectively. Treat these as claims presented in the slides; these notes do not independently verify them. [W4, slides 28-32](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

### Separate the actors

**McKinsey's conduct**

- What advice was given, on what evidence, and with what intention?
- Did repeated organizational changes benefit the client or mainly generate consulting fees?
- Were conflicts, uncertainty, and expected costs disclosed?

**HBO/Warner Bros.' conduct**

- Did management exercise independent judgment?
- Did repeated changes respect consumers, employees, creators, and shareholders?
- Did the changes build or weaken long-term power and trust?

### Applying the theories

**Kant:** A reasonable intention might be improving strategy, but an intention to prolong paid work would be harder to defend. Advice should be candid, universalizable, and respectful of the client's ability to decide.

**Machiavelli:** Fees and influence may increase a consultant's power, while failed advice may later destroy it. Rebranding may similarly gain or lose HBO's market power.

**Rand:** Rational long-term self-interest requires more than collecting fees or following prestigious advice. Both consultant and client should evaluate sustainable value and act without deception or coercion.

**Aristotle:** Relevant virtues include honesty, competence, loyalty, prudence, and courage. A consultant needs the courage to recommend no change when that is genuinely best.

The ethical quality cannot be determined from fees and name changes alone. Evidence about the advice, decision process, disclosed risks, and outcomes is necessary.

## 10. Case 8: Dark patterns and artificial scarcity

The slides show a product page stating, "In Stock: Running Low!" as an example of a possible dark pattern. [W4, slides 33-34](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Four.pdf>)

A **dark pattern** is a design choice that steers, pressures, or confuses users into decisions they might not make under clearer conditions.

### Ethical test for a scarcity message

```text
Is the stock claim accurate and current?
          ↓
Is the information useful for planning, or designed mainly to create panic?
          ↓
Can the consumer verify or meaningfully assess it?
          ↓
Would the business use the same design if manipulation were impossible?
```

- A true, proportionate warning can help consumers.
- A vague, permanently displayed, or false warning manufactures urgency.
- The ethical problem is not urgency by itself; it is deception or pressure that bypasses reflective choice.

**Kant:** Manipulative scarcity can use consumers merely as means and undermine autonomous choice.

**Utilitarianism:** Short-term sales must be compared with regret, overspending, mistrust, distorted competition, and long-term reputational harm.

**Egoism:** The design may advance immediate sales but damage rational long-term self-interest through complaints, regulation, and lost trust.

**Aristotle:** Honest persuasion can express practical wisdom; fabricated scarcity expresses dishonesty and greed.

## 11. Cross-case patterns

| Pattern | Why it matters ethically |
|---|---|
| Literal truth versus overall impression | A technically accurate fragment can still create a misleading message. |
| Intention versus foreseeable interpretation | "We meant it as a joke" does not settle how a reasonable audience would understand it. |
| Attention as power | Controversy, rage, and scarcity can create reach, but attention can also destroy trust. |
| Consumer autonomy | Deception and pressure can prevent informed, reflective choice. |
| Short-term versus long-term self-interest | Immediate sales or engagement may create regulatory and reputational losses. |
| Corporate response | Removing content is different from acknowledging wrongdoing and repairing institutional causes. |
| Ethical versus legal | A practice can be legal yet manipulative; legal restrictions can also require separate ethical justification. |

## 12. Exam-ready application template

```text
Issue
Whether [actor] acted ethically by creating/publishing/defending [specific act].

Meaning and facts
State the literal claim, likely overall impression, audience, context,
missing facts, and any assumptions.

Theory 1
State the exact doctrine and apply every element.

Theory 2
Apply a second theory that foregrounds a different moral concern.

Counterargument
Give the strongest charitable defence: humour, ambiguity, competition,
commercial interest, or lack of foreseeable harm.

Limitation
Explain why the selected theory has difficulty with these facts.

Conclusion
Classify the act as ethical/unethical outright or to a small/large extent.

Recommendation
Propose a clearer claim, review process, correction, disclosure, design change,
or accountability mechanism tied to the ethical failure.
```

## What matters most

1. Analyze the **overall impression**, not only isolated words.
2. Separate intention, impact, and the later corporate response.
3. Attention, controversy, and sales are possible benefits, but they do not automatically outweigh dignity, autonomy, truth, and trust.
4. Competition and persuasion can be ethical; deception and manipulation make them harder to defend.
5. State missing facts rather than inventing the advertiser's intention or actual financial effect.
