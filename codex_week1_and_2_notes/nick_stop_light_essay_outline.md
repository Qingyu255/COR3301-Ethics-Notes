# Outline of the Nick Stop-Light Act-Utilitarian Essay

Companion to [the sample essay](<nick_stop_light_sample_a_plus_essay.md>).

The essay uses a **nine-part issue–rule–application–evaluation–conclusion structure**. It is an expanded IRAC-style framework adapted for ethical analysis.

## Paragraph-by-paragraph outline

| Paragraph | Function | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Ethical issue** | Identifies the actor, precise act, and theory: whether Nick's running the stop light was ethical under act utilitarianism. |
| 2 | **Doctrine** | States the full utilitarian doctrine, explains its four features, and identifies the alternative action: stopping and waiting. |
| 3 | **Fact control** | Identifies missing facts and warns against inventing a collision, intoxication, or emergency. |
| 4 | **Stakeholders and benefits** | Identifies Nick's possible benefit and conditionally introduces other affected parties. |
| 5 | **Ordinary-case application** | Weighs Nick's minor convenience against foreseeable traffic risks and reaches a provisional unethical conclusion. |
| 6 | **Strong counterargument** | Tests an emergency scenario in which running the light might maximize welfare. |
| 7 | **Opposite factual branch** | Tests an intoxication-and-collision scenario, showing why consequences determine the judgment. |
| 8 | **Relevant limitation** | Criticizes the difficulty of measuring and comparing pleasure, pain, injury, and risk. |
| 9 | **Qualified conclusion** | Synthesizes both branches and concludes that the original facts are insufficient for a definitive ethical verdict. |

## Underlying argument map

```text
1. What exactly is the ethical issue?
                ↓
2. What does act utilitarianism require?
                ↓
3. What facts are known or missing?
                ↓
4. Who is affected?
                ↓
5. What pleasures and pains might result?
                ↓
6. What happens under different stated assumptions?
                ↓
7. Which alternative produces the greatest net balance?
                ↓
8. What limitation weakens our confidence?
                ↓
9. What conclusion do the available facts justify?
```

## Why the essay uses two hypothetical scenarios

The emergency and intoxication scenarios are not presented as facts. They are **conditional branches**:

```text
If ordinary time-saving:
risk of serious harm > minor convenience
→ probably unethical

If genuine emergency with precautions:
prevented suffering may exceed traffic risk
→ possibly ethical

If intoxicated and a collision occurs:
severe suffering overwhelmingly exceeds convenience
→ clearly unethical
```

This demonstrates the defining feature of act utilitarianism: outwardly similar acts can receive different judgments because their consequences differ. The branches also follow the lecturer's instruction not to rewrite the hypothetical—each added fact is expressly labelled as conditional rather than asserted.

## Reusable exam template

### 1. Issue

> The ethical issue is whether [actor's precise act] was ethical under act utilitarianism.

### 2. Doctrine

> Act utilitarianism provides that an act is right if and only if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone.

If the prompt asks you to explain the theory, briefly define consequentialism, hedonism, maximalism, and universalism. In a timed application answer, the exact doctrine and clear use of its elements are the priority; the four labels need not be mechanically defined when they add no analysis. [W1, slides 47–54](<../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

### 3. Facts and alternatives

> The facts establish that ____. However, they do not establish ____. It would therefore be inappropriate to assume ____.

Identify the main alternative to the act.

### 4. Stakeholders

> The affected parties include ____. Assuming ____, the act may also affect ____.

### 5. Benefits and harms

> The principal pleasure or avoided pain is ____. The potential painful consequences are ____. Compared with [alternative], ____ likely produces the greater net balance.

Compare the **magnitude and intensity**, not merely the number, of benefits and harms.

### 6. Counterargument or alternative branch

> However, if ____, the balance might change because ____.

Present the strongest reasonable argument against the provisional conclusion.

### 7. Relevant limitation

> This conclusion is qualified by utilitarianism's difficulty in ____. In this case, that problem matters because ____.

Possible limitations include identifying everyone affected, calculating utility, valuing consequences, subjectivity, and interpersonal comparisons of pleasure and pain. [W1, slide 75](<../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

### 8. Conclusion

> Therefore, if ____, the act would be ethical/unethical. On the facts actually provided, the most defensible conclusion is ____.

## Compressed timed-exam version

If time is limited, compress the nine functions into five paragraphs:

1. **Issue and complete doctrine.**
2. **Known facts, missing facts, stakeholders, and alternatives.**
3. **Pleasures, pains, and provisional judgment.**
4. **Strong counterargument and relevant limitation.**
5. **Clear, conditional conclusion.**

The full sample defines all four theses to demonstrate doctrinal knowledge. A shorter examination answer may combine the doctrine, act, alternative, and affected parties in two or three sentences before moving to application.

## Why the structure is strong

- It reproduces the doctrine accurately before attempting criticism.
- It identifies the precise act and available alternative.
- It considers **everyone** without presenting assumptions as facts.
- It analyzes plausible factual branches rather than silently rewriting the case.
- It compares the intensity of consequences instead of simply counting them.
- It connects a theory-specific limitation directly to the case.
- It reaches a definite conclusion about what the evidence permits: **insufficient information**, rather than automatic guilt or innocence.

The key lesson is that a qualified conclusion is not indecision. The essay determines what can responsibly be concluded from the stated facts and explains what additional facts would change that judgment.
