# Week 2: How Kantian Ethics Relates to Morality

For a shorter explanation focused specifically on Week 2 slide 4, see [Kant on Moral Obligations and Moral Law](<week2_kant_moral_obligations_explained.md>).

## Short answer

**Kantian ethics is a theory explaining what morality is, where moral duties come from, and how to decide whether an action is morally right.**

In the course's presentation of Kant, morality is not determined by whether an action produces a desirable result. It is grounded in a rational moral law that applies to everyone. A morally right action must be done with a good intention, be based on a universalizable principle, and respect persons as ends in themselves. [W2, slides 3–13](<../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

```text
Morality under Kant =
good intention
AND universalizable maxim
AND respect for persons
```

## 1. Morality concerns what we ought to do

A moral judgment is **normative**: it tells us what someone ought to do, rather than merely describing what happened.

- **Descriptive:** The manager's lie prevented the company from collapsing.
- **Moral or normative:** The manager ought not to have lied to the customers.

Kantian ethics explains how to justify the second type of claim. “The lie worked” is a description of its result; it does not by itself establish that the lie was morally right.

## 2. Morality is based on duty, not consequences

Kantian ethics is **deontological**: the morality of an action depends principally on duty, intention, and the principle governing the act—not on whether the outcome happens to be beneficial.

This contrasts with utilitarianism:

| Utilitarianism | Kantian ethics |
|---|---|
| Asks which option produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone. | Asks whether the act satisfies moral duties and respects rational persons. |
| Consequences determine rightness. | Consequences do not determine rightness. |
| A harmful-looking act might be justified by sufficiently beneficial results. | A beneficial result cannot automatically justify an immoral intention or principle. |

Suppose an engineer deliberately falsifies a safety report, but no accident occurs. The fortunate result does not erase the dishonesty under Kant. Conversely, an unforeseeable accident does not necessarily make a well-intentioned and otherwise duty-compliant act immoral. Moral judgment should not depend solely on luck.

This does not mean foreseeable consequences are irrelevant to every part of the analysis. A plan to expose people to serious harm may reveal a bad intention, an unacceptable maxim, or a failure to respect them. The narrower claim is that consequences do not by themselves determine whether the act is right.

## 3. Moral duties come from moral law

For Kant, “moral law” does not mean legislation, company policy, social convention, religious authority, or personal preference. It means a moral principle that rational agents can recognize through reason.

A genuine moral principle cannot depend on a special exception such as:

> Everyone else should be truthful, but I may lie whenever it benefits me.

That is self-serving rather than moral because the actor gives themselves a privilege that they would not rationally grant to everyone else.

The moral law is **binding**: it obliges us even when obedience is inconvenient or conflicts with our desires. It applies to all rational agents in relevantly similar circumstances, rather than changing according to status, wealth, power, or personal advantage.

## 4. Clarifying contrast: categorical rather than conditional commands

The slides describe categorical imperatives as unconditional commands. The comparison below with a hypothetical imperative is background clarification of what “unconditional” means, rather than an additional examination element.

A **hypothetical imperative** depends on a goal:

> If you want to pass the examination, you should study.

If you no longer care about passing, that instruction loses its force.

A **categorical imperative** applies regardless of what you personally want:

> Do not deceive another person merely to use them for your purposes.

Moral obligations are categorical because they do not disappear when following them becomes inconvenient.

## 5. Rationality makes universal morality possible

“All rational beings” does not mean only highly intelligent people. In this context, rational beings are agents capable of reasoning about principles, choosing how to act, and recognizing that other persons possess moral standing.

Reason allows a person to step back from immediate desire and ask:

> Could I rationally accept the principle behind my action as a rule for everyone in comparable circumstances?

This is why Kantian morality is supposed to be universal rather than a matter of individual taste.

However, rationality and morality are not identical. The slides state that moral actions are rational, but it does not follow that every rational or strategically effective action is moral. A carefully planned fraud may be instrumentally rational while still failing the moral tests. The lecturer identified this distinction as important. [W2, slides 14–15](<../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

## 6. The categorical imperatives turn moral law into tests

### Test 1: Universalizability

A **maxim** is the principle implicit in an action. Formulate it without giving the actor a personal exception, and then ask whether everyone in relevantly similar circumstances could rationally follow it.

Example maxim:

> A company may conceal known safety risks whenever disclosure would reduce its profits.

If this maxim became universal, trustworthy safety disclosure would be undermined. Companies would still depend on customers trusting their statements while adopting a principle that destroys the basis of that trust. The maxim is therefore difficult to universalize. [W2, slides 8–11](<../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

### Test 2: Respect for persons

People must be treated as ends in themselves and never **merely** as means. Rational persons possess dignity and must be allowed to make meaningful choices.

Using another person's assistance is not automatically immoral. Employing an engineer uses their skills as a means, but informed agreement, fair treatment, and freedom to refuse can also respect that person as an end.

Deception and coercion are especially problematic because they interfere with autonomous choice. A company that conceals a serious risk may use customers merely as a source of profit because customers cannot decide whether to accept the risk knowingly. [W2, slides 8 and 12–13](<../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

## 7. Good intention connects motivation to morality

The course doctrine also requires a **good intention**. An apparently beneficial act may lack moral worth if it is performed from a deceptive or exploitative intention.

For example, a company might donate to charity:

- because its decision-makers believe helping vulnerable people is morally right; or
- solely to conceal misconduct and manipulate customers.

The outward act is similar, but the intentions differ. Kantian ethics therefore asks not only, “What happened?” but also, “Why did the actor choose to do it?”

Intention must not be invented. If the hypothetical does not provide it, state the uncertainty and analyze plausible branches:

> If the manager acted to protect customers while verifying uncertain evidence, the intention may be defensible. If the manager acted to preserve profits by deceiving customers, the intention is likely bad.

### Does being morally good make something ethical under Kant?

In this module, **“moral” and “ethical” are largely interchangeable**. Kantian ethics is Kant's account of what makes an action morally right. Therefore, an action that is morally right according to Kant is ethical according to Kant.

However, “morally good” can be ambiguous. It might refer to:

- a good outcome;
- a good intention;
- a morally good person; or
- an action that satisfies Kant's complete moral test.

Only the final meaning is sufficient to establish that an act is ethical under the course's Kantian framework:

| Claim | Sufficient for Kantian ethicality? |
|---|---|
| “The action produced a good result.” | **No.** Consequences are not decisive. |
| “The actor meant well.” | **No.** Good intention is only one requirement. |
| “The actor is generally a good person.” | **No.** The particular act and its maxim must still be assessed. |
| “The act was well-intentioned, universalizable, and respectful of persons.” | **Yes**, under the course's three-part Kantian test. |

Consider a manager who lies to customers because she sincerely wants to protect them from anxiety:

- **Good intention:** Possibly satisfied because she aims to protect them.
- **Universalizability:** Possibly fails because a rule permitting protective lies could undermine truthful communication.
- **Respect for persons:** Likely fails because customers are denied information needed to make their own choices.

Her good intention therefore does not automatically make the lie ethical. Kantian analysis concerns the **particular act, its intention, and its governing maxim**, rather than merely assigning the actor a label such as “good person.” Evaluating a person's overall character is more characteristic of Aristotle's virtue ethics.

An exam-ready sentence is:

> Although the actor may have possessed a good intention, this alone does not make the action ethical under Kant. The action must also satisfy universalizability and respect for persons.

## 8. A worked example

### Case

A product manager hides a known security flaw so that users will adopt an application. Adoption provides funding for future security improvements, and no data breach occurs.

### Kantian analysis

**Good intention:** Funding future security may be a beneficial aim, but deliberately hiding the flaw suggests a deceptive intention. The apparently good goal does not automatically cleanse the chosen method.

**Universalizability:** A maxim permitting companies to hide known security flaws to increase adoption would undermine reliable disclosure and informed digital transactions if generally followed.

**Respect for persons:** Users were denied information needed to decide whether to accept the security risk. They were therefore used merely as a means of securing adoption and funding.

**Conclusion:** The concealment is unethical under Kant despite the absence of a breach. Its fortunate consequences do not repair the failures of universalizability and respect for persons.

## 9. What Kantian morality protects—and its limitation

Kantian ethics protects individuals against being sacrificed merely because doing so would increase total welfare. A person is not simply a negative number that can be outweighed by benefits to a larger group.

However, strict duties can conflict. A duty to tell the truth might appear to conflict with a duty to prevent serious harm. Because Kant makes consequences non-decisive, the theory may offer limited flexibility in emergencies. The slides identify conflicts between perfect duties and the demanding role of rationality as criticisms. [W2, slides 14–15](<../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

## Exam-ready formulation

> Kantian ethics understands morality as obedience to a rational and universally binding moral law rather than the pursuit of desirable consequences. In this module, an act is morally right only if it is done with a good intention, its maxim can be universalized, and it respects persons as ends rather than using them merely as means.

When applying Kant in an examination:

```text
1. Identify the precise act or omission.
2. Analyze the actor's intention without inventing facts.
3. Formulate and universalize the maxim.
4. Determine whether persons were respected as ends.
5. Apply all three checks even if one already fails.
6. State a supported conclusion and relevant limitation.
```

The lecturer expressly emphasized treating good intention, universalizability, and respect for persons as three separate required checks. A failure at one stage does not mean you should omit the remaining analysis (Week 2 transcript, 00:00:07–00:01:36 and 00:11:21–00:13:15).

## Compact synthesis

Kantian ethics relates to morality by claiming that:

- morality concerns **duty**, not merely successful outcomes;
- moral principles come from **reason**, not individual desire;
- genuine moral laws must apply **universally**;
- every person possesses dignity and must be respected as an **end**;
- a good result cannot automatically justify a bad intention, an inconsistent maxim, or the exploitation of another person.

In one sentence:

> **For Kant, being moral means choosing from good intention according to principles that everyone could rationally follow while respecting every person as someone with dignity and autonomy.**

A manager tells the truth only because they fear being caught lying. Their statement is accurate and helps customers. Is the act fully moral under Kant?
