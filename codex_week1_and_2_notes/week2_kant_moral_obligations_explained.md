# Week 2: Kant on Moral Obligations and Moral Law

This note unpacks the wording on Week 2 slide 4. For the complete doctrine, worked application, and exam method, see [Kantian Ethics and Morality](<week2_kantian_ethics_and_morality.md>).

The slide statement appears to contain a typo. Its intended meaning is:

> Moral obligations hold regardless of consequences. Moral obligations arise from a moral law that is **binding on** all rational beings.

This is describing **Kantian ethics**.

## 1. “Moral obligations hold regardless of consequences”

For Kant, an action does not become morally right merely because it produces a good outcome.

Compare:

- **Utilitarianism:** Was the outcome better than the alternatives?
- **Kant:** Did the person act from the right intention and according to a morally acceptable principle?

Suppose an engineer knowingly falsifies a safety report, but fortunately no accident occurs.

A consequentialist might consider the fact that nobody was harmed. Kant would say the fortunate outcome does not erase the dishonesty. We must still ask:

- What was the engineer's intention?
- Could everyone rationally follow the maxim “Falsify safety reports when disclosure is inconvenient”?
- Were users respected as autonomous persons, or deceived and used merely as a means?

Similarly, a well-intentioned act does not automatically become immoral just because it produces an unforeseeably bad outcome. Kant judges the **intention and governing moral principle**, not luck.

“Regardless of consequences” means that consequences do not determine moral rightness. It does not mean that an actor may ignore foreseeable harm: a harmful plan may reveal a bad intention, fail universalizability, or disrespect persons. The point is that a fortunate result cannot repair an act that fails Kant's moral requirements.

However, good intention by itself is not sufficient in this module:

```text
Ethical under Kant =
good intention
AND universalizable maxim
AND respect for persons
```

## 2. “Moral law”

“Moral law” does **not** mean:

- legislation passed by Parliament;
- company policy;
- religious commands;
- whatever society currently approves;
- an individual's personal feelings.

It means an objective principle of morality discoverable through **reason**.

Kant wants moral rules that are not dependent on:

- what produces the most convenient result;
- what the actor personally desires;
- who the actor is;
- whether the actor expects punishment.

For example:

> “I may lie whenever lying benefits me.”

This cannot be a genuine moral law because it gives the speaker a special exception. A moral law must be capable of applying consistently to everyone in relevantly similar circumstances.

## 3. “Binding on all rational beings”

“Binding” means **obligatory**. You must follow the moral law even when violating it would be convenient or personally beneficial.

“All rational beings” means that the requirement applies universally to persons capable of reasoning and moral choice. It does not mean “only highly intelligent people.” The relevant idea is the capacity to:

- reason about principles;
- choose how to act;
- recognize that other persons possess the same moral standing.

Because the moral law comes from reason, it cannot say:

> Nick must be honest, but I may lie because I am special.

If reason identifies honesty as a duty, then the same principle must apply impartially to all rational agents in comparable circumstances.

## 4. Categorical versus hypothetical commands

The slides describe Kant's categorical imperatives as unconditional moral commands. The hypothetical comparison below simply clarifies what “unconditional” means; it is not a fourth requirement to add to the course's three-part Kantian test.

A **hypothetical imperative** is conditional on what you want:

> If you want to pass the examination, you should study.

If you no longer want to pass, that instruction loses its force.

A **categorical imperative** is unconditional:

> Do not deceive people merely to use them for your purposes.

It applies regardless of whether honesty helps you achieve your personal goal. Moral duties are therefore categorical rather than desire-dependent.

## A useful contrast

Utilitarianism primarily asks which option produces the best consequences. Kant asks whether the intention and principle behind the action are morally acceptable and whether the means respect persons. A desirable end therefore cannot automatically justify any method used to obtain it.

## Plain-language translation

The original statement means:

> An action is not ethical simply because it ends well. We must act according to moral principles that every rational person could be required to follow, even when following them is inconvenient.

## Understanding check

A manager lies to customers to save the company. The lie succeeds, the company survives, and nobody suffers an identifiable financial loss. Does success make the lie ethical under Kant?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

No—not by itself. The outcome does not settle the Kantian judgment. We must still examine the manager's intention, whether the maxim permitting such deception could be universalized, and whether customers were respected as autonomous persons. Concealing material information may use customers merely as a means, even if the strategy succeeds.
