# Week 2: How to Argue Whether an Act Is Ethical Under the Three Strands of Ethical Egoism

## 1. Start with the core idea

Ethical egoism is **normative**:

> Individuals **ought** to act in self-interested ways.

This is different from the descriptive claim that people often behave selfishly. A description of how people behave does not prove how they ought to behave. [W2, slides 16–18](<../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

The three strands do not use one identical definition of self-interest:

| Strand | What makes the act ethical? |
|---|---|
| **Machiavelli** | It acquires or preserves the actor's power to dominate others. |
| **Hobbes** | Cooperation advances the actor's self-interest. |
| **Ayn Rand** | The act rationally advances the actor's long-term self-interest through uncoerced personal choice. |

[W2, slides 18–28](<../week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

The basic exam method is:

```text
Exact strand-specific doctrine
→ precise actor and act
→ relevant form of self-interest
→ supporting and opposing facts
→ short-term and long-term analysis
→ strand-specific conclusion
→ relevant limitation
```

Do not write merely:

> The act benefited the actor, so it was ethical under egoism.

That is too general. Explain **what kind of benefit** counts under the selected strand and why the facts satisfy—or fail—its test.

### Keep the actor fixed

Identify whose act is being judged and do not switch perspectives halfway through. If the issue concerns a CEO's act, a benefit to the company matters only insofar as it advances the CEO's relevant self-interest. If the company itself is treated as the actor, say so expressly and explain what counts as its interest. Do not aggregate everyone's welfare; that would turn the analysis into utilitarianism.

## 2. Machiavelli: power and domination

### Course doctrine

> An act is right if it is done to dominate, or to maintain the power to dominate, others.

The relevant form of self-interest is **power**. Money, popularity, cooperation, or kindness matter only if they help the actor acquire or preserve power to dominate others.

### Application steps

```text
1. Identify the actor and precise act.
2. Ask whether the act was directed toward gaining or preserving power.
3. Explain whom the actor can dominate or influence as a result.
4. Test whether the power lasts in the long term.
5. Conclude whether the act advances Machiavellian self-interest.
```

### Questions to ask

- Does the act increase the actor's authority, influence, control, reputation, or strategic leverage?
- Does it prevent rivals from weakening the actor?
- Does apparently generous conduct create loyalty or dependence?
- Is the gain only temporary?
- Might resentment, rebellion, exposure, or retaliation ultimately destroy the actor's power?

Do not assume that a cruel action is automatically Machiavellian. Cruelty that provokes the actor's downfall may fail to preserve power. Similarly, kindness can be Machiavellian if it builds loyalty and control.

### Worked example: a CEO funds employee education

**Argument that the act is ethical:** Funding education may increase employee loyalty, strengthen the CEO's reputation, and make employees more dependent on the organization for advancement. If these effects consolidate the CEO's authority and organizational influence, the act advances the power to dominate and is ethical under Machiavelli.

**Counterargument:** Better-qualified employees may leave for competitors or become capable of challenging the CEO. If the programme weakens the CEO's long-term control, its apparently positive short-term effects would not make it ethical under this strand.

**Conclusion:** The act is ethical under Machiavelli if the stronger conclusion from the facts is that it secures durable power. Whether employees benefit is not decisive by itself.

### Exam paragraph template

> Machiavellian egoism provides that an act is right if it is done to dominate, or maintain the power to dominate, others. [Actor's] act of [precise act] may be ethical because it enables [actor] to acquire or preserve power by [fact-specific mechanism]. In the short term, ____. However, in the long term, ____ may strengthen/weaken that power. On balance, because the act [does/does not] secure durable power to dominate, it is [ethical/unethical] under Machiavelli.

### Relevant criticism

Machiavelli's account assumes a world of rivalry and mutual animosity. Treating domination as ethically decisive may undermine trust and overlook relationships that are not power struggles. Apply that criticism to the facts rather than merely attaching it as a generic sentence.

Example:

> In this workplace, employees appear to cooperate voluntarily rather than compete for domination. Machiavelli may therefore mischaracterize a relationship based on trust as a struggle for control.

## 3. Hobbes: cooperation for self-interest

### Course doctrine

> Cooperation is right when that cooperation is in the actor's self-interest.

Hobbes does not claim that cooperation is inherently moral or altruistic. People cooperate because doing so protects them or enables them to obtain something they cannot secure as effectively through conflict.

### Application steps

```text
1. Identify the actor and the cooperative act or arrangement.
2. Identify what each side contributes.
3. Identify the benefit the actor receives through cooperation.
4. Compare cooperation with conflict, refusal, or acting alone.
5. Ask whether enforcement or reliable compliance is needed.
6. Conclude whether cooperation advances the actor's self-interest.
```

### Questions to ask

- Who must cooperate with whom?
- What does the actor gain from the arrangement?
- What would happen to the actor if cooperation broke down?
- Is the benefit reciprocal, even if it is unequal?
- Is the actor cooperating voluntarily, strategically, or because rules are enforced?
- Would refusing to cooperate actually harm the actor's long-term welfare?

The fact that other people also benefit does not defeat Hobbesian egoism. Mutual benefit is often the mechanism through which the actor advances their own interests.

### Worked example: a CEO funds employee education

**Argument that the act is ethical:** The company supplies training and employees contribute improved skills, productivity, and continued service. This cooperation helps the CEO maintain a capable and stable workforce, advancing the CEO's long-term interests. The fact that employees also benefit is compatible with Hobbesian egoism.

**Counterargument:** If the training creates no meaningful benefit for the CEO or company, or employees accept the benefit without contributing to the cooperative arrangement, the connection to the CEO's self-interest is weakened. The act might be generous, but Hobbesian egoism would not classify it as ethical merely because it helps others.

**Conclusion:** The programme is ethical under Hobbes if reciprocal cooperation produces a real advantage for the CEO compared with refusing to cooperate or acting alone.

### Exam paragraph template

> Hobbesian egoism provides that cooperation is right when that cooperation is in the actor's self-interest. [Actor] cooperated with [other party] by ____. This arrangement advances [actor's] interest because ____, whereas refusing to cooperate would likely ____. Conversely, ____. On balance, because the cooperation [does/does not] protect or advance [actor's] long-term welfare, it is [ethical/unethical] under Hobbes.

### Relevant criticism

Hobbes may overstate the danger of non-cooperation. Social collapse or severe conflict is not always the realistic alternative.

Example:

> The theory assumes that the group must cooperate to succeed, but the facts suggest that the employee could complete the task independently. Hobbes may therefore exaggerate the necessity of cooperation in this case.

### Coercion under Hobbes

The lecturer treated coercion as more ambiguous under Hobbes than under Rand. Enforced cooperation may still protect the actor's interests, but an actor who is forced may not genuinely be choosing cooperation for self-interest. If coercion is present, explain both possibilities rather than importing Rand's automatic rule into Hobbes.

## 4. Ayn Rand: rational, long-term and uncoerced self-interest

### Course doctrine

> An act is right if it rationally advances one's self-interest.

For the course, Rand requires more than whatever the actor currently wants. The important elements are:

```text
ethical under Rand =
rational advancement of self-interest
AND long-term well-being or happiness
AND uncoerced personal choice
```

Preservation of the actor's life is especially important. A whim, instinct, addiction, or obviously self-destructive choice is not rational self-interest merely because the actor desires it.

### Application steps

```text
1. Identify the actor and precise act.
2. Determine whether the choice was uncoerced.
3. Identify the interest the actor seeks to advance.
4. Ask whether the act is rational rather than impulsive or self-destructive.
5. Compare short-term gratification with long-term well-being.
6. Consider whether the act preserves the actor's life and capacity to flourish.
7. Conclude whether it rationally advances the actor's interest.
```

### Questions to ask

- Did the actor choose freely, or were they forced?
- Is the claimed benefit real or merely perceived?
- Does the act promote lasting happiness and well-being?
- Does it expose the actor to foreseeable ruin, imprisonment, addiction, injury, or death?
- Were safer or more sustainable alternatives available?
- Does helping another person also provide the actor with freely chosen fulfilment or advantage?

Other people's interests can matter, but the act must connect to the actor's own rational self-interest. A person may help another because friendship, fulfilment, loyalty, or reciprocal benefit contributes to their own happiness.

### Worked example: a CEO funds employee education

**Argument that the act is ethical:** If the CEO freely chooses the programme after rationally determining that a skilled workforce, organizational success, and personal fulfilment advance long-term well-being, the act is ethical under Rand. Employee benefit does not disqualify it.

**Counterargument:** If the programme threatens the firm's survival, is adopted impulsively, or is imposed on the CEO through coercion, it may not constitute rational and uncoerced self-interest. A pleasant public image alone may be an inadequate long-term benefit.

**Conclusion:** The act is ethical under Rand only if it is both rationally beneficial in the long term and freely chosen.

### Exam paragraph template

> Rand's egoism provides that an act is right if it rationally advances one's self-interest. [Actor's] act of ____ was [uncoerced/coerced] because ____. It appears to advance [actor's] interest by ____. Although it may produce the short-term benefit of ____, its long-term effects include ____. Because the act [does/does not] rationally promote the actor's freely chosen long-term happiness and well-being, it is [ethical/unethical] under Rand.

### Relevant criticism

Rand claims that rational self-interest can contribute to general prosperity, but scarce resources create a difficulty. Several people may rationally pursue incompatible interests, and not everyone can necessarily succeed.

Example:

> Each executive may rationally pursue the same limited promotion, but only one can obtain it. The case therefore challenges the claim that everyone's pursuit of self-interest will reliably generate prosperity for all.

## 5. The same act under all three strands

### Case

A manager shares valuable technical training with employees.

| Strand | What must be proved? | Possible ethical argument |
|---|---|---|
| **Machiavelli** | The training helps the manager acquire or preserve power. | It builds loyalty, influence, and dependence, strengthening the manager's authority. |
| **Hobbes** | Cooperation benefits the manager. | Employees gain skills while the manager gains a more effective and stable team. |
| **Rand** | The decision rationally advances long-term self-interest and is uncoerced. | The manager freely invests in long-term organizational success and personal fulfilment. |

The three arguments reach the same conclusion for different reasons:

```text
Same action: sharing training

Machiavelli → ethical because it creates power
Hobbes       → ethical because cooperation benefits the actor
Rand         → ethical because it is rational, long-term, and freely chosen
```

Do not merge these into the vague statement that “training is ethical because it is beneficial.” Preserve the distinctive test used by each strand.

### A case where the strands may disagree

A manager forces employees to work unpaid weekends to complete an important project.

- **Machiavelli:** The act might be ethical if it demonstrates authority and preserves the manager's power. However, resentment, resignations, or collective resistance could erode that power in the long term.
- **Hobbes:** If the arrangement is genuine cooperation that protects the manager's interests and prevents shared failure, it may be defensible. Calling unilateral coercion “cooperation,” however, is contestable, especially if it destroys future trust.
- **Rand:** Coercion is a direct problem under the course's presentation of Rand. The employees' choices are not uncoerced, and the manager may also incur irrational long-term damage to the workforce. The act is therefore more likely unethical under Rand.

Disagreement is not a mistake. It shows that the strands define self-interest differently.

## 6. How to choose the most relevant strand

Use factual triggers:

```text
Power, control, political strategy, domination, rivals
→ Machiavelli

Mutual dependence, contracts, teamwork, enforced order, avoiding conflict
→ Hobbes

Coercion, personal choice, rationality, addiction, survival, long-term welfare
→ Ayn Rand
```

The lecturer emphasized knowing the differences and choosing the theory that fits the facts rather than the theory that looks most difficult (Week 2 transcript, 00:29:05–00:30:33, 00:34:46–00:36:34, and 00:55:32–00:55:38).

Unless the question expressly asks for all three strands, you do not necessarily need to apply all three. A complete and accurate application of the most relevant strand is stronger than three shallow paragraphs. If you do use all three, analyze each independently and give a separate conclusion under each.

## 7. Common exam mistakes

### Mistake 1: treating egoism as descriptive

Weak:

> The CEO acted selfishly, so ethical egoism applies.

Better:

> Ethical egoism is normative: the issue is whether the CEO ought to have acted in the relevant form of self-interest, not merely whether the CEO happened to behave selfishly.

### Mistake 2: assuming self-interest means short-term pleasure

A profitable act may destroy reputation or power later. An enjoyable act may threaten health or life. Examine the long term, especially under Machiavelli and Rand.

### Mistake 3: assuming helping others contradicts egoism

An egoist may help others when doing so creates power, valuable cooperation, reciprocal benefit, fulfilment, or happiness for the actor.

### Mistake 4: considering everyone's welfare as if applying utilitarianism

Other people matter under egoism primarily through their relationship to the actor's self-interest. If you impartially aggregate everyone's welfare, you have switched to utilitarianism.

### Mistake 5: treating all three strands as interchangeable

Power is not the same as cooperation, and cooperation is not the same as rational uncoerced choice. State the applicable doctrine precisely.

### Mistake 6: deciding that an act is ethical merely because the actor wanted it

This is particularly weak under Rand. Desire can be irrational, coerced, addictive, impulsive, or destructive of long-term welfare.

### Mistake 7: criticizing before completing the doctrine

First apply the chosen strand and reach its internal conclusion. Only then explain why its account of ethicality may be inadequate.

## 8. A reusable comparative answer structure

If an examination expressly asks you to apply all three strands, use:

### Introduction

> The ethical issue is whether [actor's act] was ethical under ethical egoism. Ethical egoism normatively requires individuals to act in self-interested ways, but Machiavelli, Hobbes, and Rand define the relevant self-interest differently.

### Machiavelli paragraph

```text
Doctrine
→ power gained or preserved
→ short-term and long-term threat to power
→ conclusion
```

### Hobbes paragraph

```text
Doctrine
→ cooperative arrangement
→ benefit to actor versus non-cooperation
→ conclusion
```

### Rand paragraph

```text
Doctrine
→ coercion check
→ rationality check
→ long-term welfare and preservation of life
→ conclusion
```

### Synthesis

> Machiavelli would judge the act ____, because ____. Hobbes would judge it ____, because ____. Rand would judge it ____, because ____. Their disagreement arises because Machiavelli equates self-interest with power, Hobbes with beneficial cooperation, and Rand with rational, long-term, uncoerced choice. On the facts, ____ is the most persuasive/relevant analysis because ____.

Do not “vote” among the three theories by counting conclusions. Explain which moral interpretation best fits the facts and why.

## 9. Final scoring checklist

Before finishing an egoism answer, ask:

- Did I say ethical egoism is **normative**?
- Did I identify the actor and precise act?
- Did I state the exact doctrine of the selected strand?
- Did I explain the relevant type of self-interest?
- Did I consider short-term and long-term effects where relevant?
- For Machiavelli, did I connect the act to power and domination?
- For Hobbes, did I identify cooperation and its benefit to the actor?
- For Rand, did I analyze rationality, voluntariness, coercion, and long-term well-being?
- Did I avoid converting egoism into utilitarianism?
- Did I reach a strand-specific conclusion?
- Did I connect any criticism directly to the facts?

### One-sentence memory aid

> **Machiavelli asks whether the act builds power; Hobbes asks whether cooperation benefits the actor; Rand asks whether the actor freely and rationally advances long-term self-interest.**

### Understanding check

A student joins a group project only because working with others is necessary for a high grade. Their teammates also benefit. How could the act be assessed under each strand?

<details>
<summary>Suggested answer</summary>

- **Machiavelli:** The act is ethical only if joining helps the student acquire or preserve influence or power over others. Receiving a high grade alone does not necessarily satisfy the power-based test.
- **Hobbes:** The act is likely ethical because cooperation with teammates advances the student's interest in obtaining a high grade.
- **Rand:** The act may be ethical if the student freely and rationally chooses cooperation as a sustainable way to advance long-term educational interests. If the participation is coerced in the relevant sense, or if the strategy is irrationally self-destructive, the conclusion changes.

The teammates' benefit does not disqualify the act under egoism. What matters is how the cooperation relates to the student's own self-interest under each strand.
