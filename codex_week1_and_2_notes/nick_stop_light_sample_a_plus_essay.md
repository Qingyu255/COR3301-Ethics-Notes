# Sample High-Scoring Essay: Act Utilitarianism

## Whether Nick Acted Ethically by Running a Stop Light

The ethical issue is whether Nick's act of running the stop light was ethical under act utilitarianism.

Act utilitarianism provides that an action is right if and only if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone. It is consequentialist because the act is judged by its consequences; hedonistic because pleasure—or the avoidance of pain—is treated as good; maximalist because the chosen act must produce the greatest net balance compared with the alternatives; and universalist because the consequences for everyone affected must be considered impartially. The relevant act is Nick's running of the stop light. The principal alternative is for Nick to stop and wait. [W1, slides 47–54 and 58–62](<../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

The facts are extremely limited. They do not reveal why Nick ran the stop light, whether other road users were present, whether he created an immediate danger, or what consequences actually followed. These omissions are material because act utilitarianism evaluates the particular consequences of the particular act. It would therefore be inappropriate to assume that Nick was drunk, struck a pedestrian, or caused an accident. Doing so would change the hypothetical rather than analyze it.

Nick is the only stakeholder whose involvement is certain. Running the stop light might have allowed him to save time or reach his destination sooner, producing some pleasure or avoiding the inconvenience of waiting. However, this benefit appears relatively minor unless Nick was responding to an urgent situation. It is also reasonable—but must be identified as an assumption—to consider other motorists, cyclists, pedestrians, passengers, and emergency services if they were present or foreseeably exposed to the act.

Assuming other road users were present, Nick's action created a risk of collision, physical injury, property damage, fear, and disruption. These painful consequences could be severe even if their probability was uncertain. Nick himself could also have been injured or subjected to legal penalties. Compared with these possible harms, the ordinary benefit of saving a small amount of time would probably be insufficient. On that assumption, stopping would likely have produced a greater balance of pleasure over pain, making Nick's act unethical.

Nevertheless, a different conclusion could follow if the missing facts were materially different. Suppose, purely as an illustrative conditional rather than an asserted fact, that Nick was a police officer responding to an ongoing murder and that crossing the junction could substantially reduce the risk to a victim. The benefit of arriving sooner might then outweigh the traffic risk, particularly if Nick used lights, sirens, and appropriate precautions. In those circumstances, running the stop light could produce the greatest net balance and therefore be ethical.

Conversely, if Nick was intoxicated and struck a pedestrian, the severe injuries, fear, property damage, emergency response costs, and suffering experienced by the pedestrian and others would overwhelmingly exceed Nick's convenience. His act would clearly be unethical under act utilitarianism. These contrasting scenarios demonstrate why outwardly identical conduct can receive different act-utilitarian judgments: the theory evaluates consequences, not merely the physical description of the act.

The analysis must also compare the magnitude of pleasure and pain rather than simply count them. One significant injury could outweigh several minor conveniences. Yet this exposes a limitation of utilitarianism: pleasure, pain, risk, and injury do not share an objective unit of measurement. Their intensity may be experienced differently by different people, and it may be impossible to identify everyone indirectly affected. Consequently, even with additional facts, the calculation would retain some subjectivity. [W1, slide 75](<../week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

In conclusion, the statement that "Nick ran a stop light" is insufficient to determine whether he acted ethically under act utilitarianism. If he merely sought to save time while exposing others to significant danger, the painful consequences would likely outweigh the pleasure and his act would be unethical. If he acted during a genuine emergency and produced a substantially greater benefit while minimizing risk, the act might be ethical. On the facts actually provided, however, the most defensible conclusion is that Nick's ethicality remains indeterminate because the consequences, affected parties, and alternatives have not been sufficiently established. This qualified conclusion follows the stated facts without silently rewriting them.

## Why This Is Strong

- It reproduces the doctrine accurately.
- It identifies the precise act and alternative.
- It considers "everyone" without inventing stakeholders as facts.
- It labels assumptions and analyzes both branches.
- It compares the magnitude—not merely the number—of consequences.
- It applies a relevant criticism to the case.
- It reaches a definite conclusion about what the available facts permit: **insufficient information**, not automatic guilt or innocence.

> **Timed-exam adaptation:** This full sample defines all four utilitarian theses to make its doctrinal reasoning visible. If the examination prompt is mainly application and time is limited, state the exact doctrine once, identify the act and alternatives, and move promptly to stakeholders and consequences. Do not sacrifice fact-specific analysis merely to reproduce every label.
