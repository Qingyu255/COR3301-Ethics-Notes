# Week 3 Notes: Applying Ethics to Real-World Cases

## What Week 3 is really about

Week 3 does not mainly introduce a new ethical theory. It asks a harder question:

> How do we use the theories from Weeks 1 and 2 when every realistic option has benefits, harms, uncertainty, and hidden consequences?

The lecture moves through four kinds of case:

1. a group presentation about developing **Maju Forest**;
2. the story of **Kim Bok-Dong**, a survivor of the Japanese military "comfort women" system;
3. environmental choices involving **plastic straws, drink cartons, and cotton tote bags**; and
4. *The Good Place* and the difficulty of living a perfectly ethical life in a complex economy.

The unifying lesson is:

```text
Good label or good intention
            ↓
is not enough
            ↓
Identify the precise act, actor, stakeholders, consequences,
intention, power relations, character, and relevant duties
            ↓
Compare realistic alternatives and give a qualified conclusion
```

## 1. The analysis habits being tested

### 1.1 Separate the acts

Do not evaluate an entire country, company, or product as though it performed one action. Frame each issue as:

> Whether **[actor]** acted ethically by **[precise act or omission]**.

Examples:

- whether a government acted ethically by authorizing a coercive wartime system;
- whether individual soldiers acted ethically by participating in it;
- whether a modern government acted ethically in its acknowledgment or settlement;
- whether a company acted ethically by using a drink carton;
- whether it acted ethically by labelling that carton "recyclable";
- whether a consumer acted ethically by acquiring another tote bag;
- whether that consumer acted ethically by repeatedly using an existing tote bag.

Different acts can receive different ethical judgments. The lecturer emphasized separating actors and actions during the Kim Bok-Dong discussion. (Week 3 transcript 2, approximately 00:17:05-00:17:39.)

### 1.2 A good intention does not settle the case

Under Kantian ethics, all three parts of the course doctrine must be considered:

```text
good intention
AND universalizable maxim
AND respect for persons
```

An environmental campaign may begin with good intentions yet fail because its practice cannot be universalized or because people are misled. Likewise, even if a government asserts a beneficial purpose, that does not erase coercion or disrespect for persons. (W3, slides 18-19; transcript 2, approximately 00:39:39-00:42:19.)

### 1.3 Ignorance does not erase consequences

For utilitarianism, an actual benefit or harm still enters the balance even if the actor did not know about it. Lack of knowledge may matter to intention or voluntariness, but it does not make the consequence disappear. (Week 3 transcript 1, approximately 00:47:29-00:48:20; transcript 2, approximately 00:39:59-00:40:10.)

### 1.4 Do not force every theory into every case

Use the theories that illuminate the facts most clearly. For Aristotle, identify the most relevant virtue and its deficiency and excess; you do not need to discuss every virtue. A theory can also be a poor fit, and explaining that limitation is part of good analysis. (Week 3 transcript 1, approximately 00:45:42-00:47:08.)

## 2. Group-presentation case: developing Maju Forest

> **Source note:** This case appears in the first transcript, but the group's own slides are not among the supplied files. The conclusions below summarize the arguments discussed in class; they are not a single official answer from the lecturer.

### The conflict

Developing the forest may provide housing, construction activity, amenities, and infrastructure. Preserving it may protect biodiversity, wildlife habitat, cooling, water retention, recreation, community identity, and options for future generations.

Important stakeholders include:

- people seeking housing;
- nearby residents;
- government planners and taxpayers;
- workers and businesses connected to construction;
- environmental researchers and nature groups;
- future residents and future generations; and
- wildlife and ecosystems.

### Applying the theories

| Lens | Argument supporting development | Argument opposing or limiting development |
|---|---|---|
| Act utilitarianism | More housing, jobs, facilities, and possible improvements to the area create welfare. | Habitat loss, warming, disruption, community loss, and irreversible ecological damage may outweigh the immediate gains. |
| Rule utilitarianism | A qualified rule allowing development after genuine need and rigorous impact review may support social welfare. | "Always clear" and "never clear" are both too rigid. The rule must be general and compared with alternatives. |
| Kant | Providing needed housing may reflect a defensible intention. | A maxim allowing significant forests to be cleared whenever demand rises may be difficult to universalize. A merely performative consultation may treat residents as means. |
| Machiavelli | Controlling land-use decisions may acquire or preserve governmental power. | Public backlash, loss of legitimacy, and forced compromise may weaken long-term power. |
| Hobbes | Government may coordinate competing groups to secure order and welfare. | Calling imposed policy "cooperation" can hide unequal power or the absence of real consent. |
| Rand | Long-term, rational planning could advance the state's or citizens' interests. | Short-term development that undermines long-term livability is not rational self-interest. Rand may be a weaker fit when the actor is a collective government rather than an individual. |
| Aristotle | Practical wisdom (*phronesis*), justice, moderation, and responsible statecraft may support a balanced plan. | Virtue ethics does not calculate exactly how much forest preservation is the correct mean. The analyst must justify, not merely assert, the balance. |
| Confucianism | Government can be analogized to a ruler with a duty to provide for the community and pursue public good. | "Public good" is contestable. Modern democratic citizens are not simply passive subjects, and insufficient participation may damage good governance. |

### What the case teaches

- **Future generations count** under utilitarianism even if they never knew the particular forest existed. They may still experience ecological consequences.
- **Irreversibility matters:** houses can be designed differently or built elsewhere, while a mature ecosystem may be much harder to restore.
- **Intentions must be labelled as inferences:** a government's inner intention is rarely observable; it must be inferred from its policies and conduct.
- **Consultation quality matters:** inviting comments after a decision is effectively fixed is not the same as allowing meaningful participation.
- **A compromise is not automatically the ethical mean:** under Aristotle, explain why the proposed balance expresses the relevant virtues.

The strongest general answer is conditional: development becomes more defensible when housing need is evidenced, alternatives are genuinely compared, ecological harm is minimized, affected parties can influence the decision, and the remaining benefits plausibly exceed the long-term loss.

## 3. Kim Bok-Dong: coercion, dignity, and responsibility

The lecture asks students to assess the actions of the Japanese government, individual soldiers, Kim Bok-Dong, and relevant later governments separately. [W3, slides 4-6](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Three (1).pdf>)

### A careful issue map

```text
Wartime government → creating, authorizing, or maintaining the system
Individual soldiers → participating in coercive sexual exploitation
Later governments  → acknowledgment, education, settlement, and repair
Kim Bok-Dong        → questioning authorities and seeking recognition
```

### Theory comparison

| Theory | Main judgment or question |
|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | Any limited gratification or claimed military benefit is overwhelmed by captivity, sexual violence, fear, injury, trauma, stigma, family harm, and long-term international mistrust. |
| Kant | The victims were denied autonomy and used merely as means. A maxim permitting military sexual exploitation cannot be rationally universalized. This is the clearest framework for explaining the violation of dignity. |
| Machiavelli | Short-term domination might initially look power-preserving, but long-term resistance, condemnation, and regional animosity can reverse the egoist result. The initial result also exposes how domination-centered egoism can disregard victims. |
| Hobbes | Cooperation among officials or soldiers does not turn coercion of captives into genuine cooperation. This strand is therefore a weak justification of the conduct. |
| Rand | Coercion is incompatible with the course's emphasis on uncoerced choice, and the long-term loss of legitimacy may undermine rational self-interest. |
| Aristotle | The conduct expresses injustice, cruelty, and lack of restraint. Duress may affect an individual soldier's responsibility, but it does not make the exploitation virtuous. |
| Confucianism | Authority brings reciprocal duties of humane care. Exploiting vulnerable people is an abuse of unequal power. Kant still offers clearer protection of individual rights and autonomy. |

### Is questioning one's government ethical?

Questioning authority is not automatically disloyal or unethical. If a ruler or government fails its duty to care for people, criticism can seek accountability, public good, acknowledgment, and prevention of future harm. Under Confucian analysis, obedience should not be detached from the ruler's reciprocal duties. Under Kant, allowing survivors to speak and participate respects them as persons rather than objects of diplomatic settlement.

For a fuller worked answer, see [Ethical Analysis of Madam Kim's Interview](<../study-guides/case-studies/madam-kim-comfort-women-analysis.md>).

## 4. Environmental ethics and sustainability

### The central puzzle

Humans have a major adverse effect on the environment, but attempted solutions can create additional problems. [W3, slide 8](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Three (1).pdf>)

This means environmental reasoning should examine a product's **life cycle**, not merely its label:

```text
raw materials → production → transport → use and reuse → disposal → recycling/downcycling
```

A product is not necessarily ethical merely because it is:

- reusable;
- recyclable in principle;
- made from a natural material;
- marketed as plastic-free; or
- purchased with good intentions.

Ask instead: compared with what realistic alternative, under what pattern of use, and with which total effects?

## 5. Case study: plastic versus paper or bioplastic straws

The slides state that plastic straws can persist for more than 300 years, while paper and bioplastic straws are imperfect substitutes. They also remind us that plastic packaging is a larger problem than straws alone. [W3, slides 9-10](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Three (1).pdf>)

### Why this is an ethical dilemma

Plastic straws can be cheap, convenient, durable, and important for some users, including people with accessibility needs. Their environmental costs include persistent waste and harm to wildlife. Alternatives may require more resources, perform poorly, cost more, or create other forms of waste.

The right comparison is not simply:

```text
plastic = bad     paper = good
```

It is:

```text
plastic straw
vs paper/bioplastic/reusable straw
vs no straw where feasible
considering production, accessibility, use, and disposal
```

### Theory applications

- **Utilitarianism:** compare convenience, accessibility, cost, environmental harm, and all affected parties. Do not count consequences mechanically; compare their scale and intensity.
- **Kant:** test a maxim such as "Consumers may use disposable products whenever they are slightly more convenient." Also ask whether an absolute ban fails to respect people who genuinely require accessible straws.
- **Aristotle:** examine moderation, responsibility, and practical wisdom. Mindless overconsumption may show excess, while a rigid refusal that disregards another person's needs may fail compassion.
- **Confucianism:** consider reciprocal responsibility among consumers, businesses, government, affected communities, and future generations.

### Scope matters

The lecturer raised an important framing question: perhaps using the straw is not the only issue; disposal may be a separate act. Analyze the exact issue asked rather than quietly expanding or narrowing it. (Week 3 transcript 2, approximately 00:31:43-00:32:23.)

## 6. Case study: "boxed" water and drink cartons

Cartons look environmentally friendly because many display a recycling logo and are perceived as alternatives to plastic bottles. The slides complicate that impression:

- cartons combine several materials and are harder to recycle;
- they cannot be completely recycled;
- few facilities have the required technology; and
- recycling may actually be **downcycling** into a product that cannot be recycled again.

[W3, slides 11-15](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Three (1).pdf>)

### Three separate ethical issues

1. Should the business continue using the carton?
2. Is the carton better than the realistic packaging alternatives?
3. Is it ethical to label the carton "recyclable" when ordinary consumers may misunderstand what that means?

The third is not just an environmental-impact question. It is also a question about honesty, informed choice, and possible **greenwashing**.

### Strongest theory fit

- **Utilitarianism:** compare the carton with actual alternatives across production, transport, use, and local disposal capacity.
- **Kant:** a technically true but predictably misleading recycling claim may manipulate consumers and use them as a means to sales. Ask whether transparent labelling could be universalized and whether it supports autonomous choice.
- **Aristotle:** examine honesty, temperance, responsibility, and whether the company's conduct reflects stable good character or image management.
- **Confucianism:** profit cannot be the sole aim; the company has duties to consumers and the wider community.

Do not jump from "hard to recycle" to "plastic bottles are better." The slides expose uncertainty; they do not provide a full comparative life-cycle assessment.

## 7. Case study: reusable cotton tote bags

The lecture challenges the intuition that "reusable" automatically means "sustainable." The slides report that:

- an organic cotton tote bag may need **20,000 uses** to offset its overall production impact;
- only 15% of 30 million tons of cotton produced each year reach facilities that recycle textiles; and
- 10-15% of recycled tote bags may be wasted because PVC-based logo dyes are difficult to break down.

[W3, slides 16-20](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Three (1).pdf>)

Treat these figures as claims in the course slides. Their real-world application still depends on what alternatives, impacts, and recycling systems are being compared.

### The key distinction

```text
Using an existing tote repeatedly
is not the same act as
acquiring another tote that may rarely be used.
```

Similarly, "using a tote" and "avoiding a plastic bag on this occasion" are related but separable issues. (Week 3 transcript 2, approximately 00:40:39-00:41:09.)

### Intention versus result

The shift toward reusable bags may have had good intentions. But businesses may also use tote bags for branding, and good intention does not establish good consequences. [W3, slide 19](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Three (1).pdf>)

- **Utilitarianism:** focus on actual life-cycle consequences and how often the bag is used.
- **Kant:** test the maxim and the honesty of sustainability claims; intention alone is insufficient.
- **Aristotle:** distinguish responsible reuse from status-driven accumulation. Relevant virtues include moderation, practical wisdom, and honesty.
- **Egoism:** a company may distribute bags for reputation or sales. That can fit self-interest, but ask whether backlash and waste undermine long-term interest.
- **Confucianism:** ask whether the business contributes to public good or merely uses environmental concern to accumulate profit.

Practical conclusion: the most defensible default is usually to use what one already owns for as long as practical rather than repeatedly acquiring new "green" products.

## 8. *The Good Place*: why perfect ethical consumption seems impossible

The clips show a point system that assigns moral value to actions by considering resource use, intention, and effects on others. The problem is that modern choices have many indirect consequences. Buying a tomato can implicate pesticides, labour exploitation, transport emissions, and other supply-chain effects that the buyer may not know about. [W3, slides 21-24](<Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Three (1).pdf>); Week 3 transcript 2, approximately 00:51:40-00:58:24.

### The philosophical problem

```text
People appear to make one simple choice
                    ↓
but complex supply chains embed many other choices
                    ↓
complete knowledge and zero impact may be impossible
```

This creates tension between:

- **consequences:** harms occur even when unknown;
- **intention:** a person may genuinely try to do good;
- **knowledge and voluntariness:** responsibility may be reduced when facts are unknowable;
- **fairness:** demanding perfect research from every consumer may be unrealistic; and
- **institutional responsibility:** businesses and governments often possess more information and power than individual consumers.

The lecture's question is not an excuse to stop trying. It is a prompt to replace moral purity with reasoned, proportionate responsibility.

## 9. What would each theory call a sustainable life?

| Theory | A sustainable life would emphasize... | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | choices and rules that maximize long-term welfare for everyone affected, including future people and environmental consequences | impacts are difficult to predict, measure, and compare |
| Kant | universalizable practices, honest claims, and respect for the autonomy and dignity of people affected by production and consumption | the course's respect-for-persons principle protects rational persons more directly than ecosystems |
| Machiavelli | environmental action that acquires or preserves power and legitimacy | may value sustainability only instrumentally |
| Hobbes | cooperation on environmental rules when cooperation advances each party's long-term interest | can struggle where actors benefit from free-riding |
| Rand | rational, voluntary choices that preserve long-term life and flourishing | individual self-interest may not reliably solve collective environmental harms |
| Aristotle | moderation, responsibility, justice, and practical wisdom developed as habits | no precise formula locates the correct environmental "mean" |
| Confucianism | reciprocal care, responsible leadership, community welfare, and duties within relationships | hierarchy and a weak rights doctrine may underprotect individuals or outsiders |

No theory necessarily demands a literally zero-impact life. Each changes what counts as the morally important reason for reducing harm.

## 10. A reusable Week 3 case method

Use this for any environmental or social-responsibility problem:

```text
1. ISSUE
   Whether [actor] acted ethically by [precise act/omission].

2. FACTS AND UNCERTAINTIES
   What is stated? What must be inferred? What information is missing?

3. LIFE CYCLE / CAUSAL CHAIN
   Production → transport → use → reuse → disposal → long-term effects.

4. STAKEHOLDERS
   Include indirect groups, vulnerable parties, future generations,
   and non-human life where the theory permits.

5. REALISTIC ALTERNATIVES
   Compare the proposed action with feasible substitutes—not perfection.

6. THEORY
   State the exact doctrine and apply every element.

7. COUNTERARGUMENT AND LIMITATION
   Give the strongest opposing case and explain what the theory misses.

8. CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATION
   Give a qualified judgment and a practical improvement.
```

### Common mistakes

- assuming natural, paper, cotton, reusable, or recyclable automatically means ethical;
- treating good intention as sufficient under Kant;
- ignoring harms merely because the actor did not know about them;
- comparing a real option with an imaginary perfect option;
- counting the number of utilitarian consequences without considering severity;
- treating a compromise as Aristotle's mean without identifying a virtue;
- calling coercion cooperation under Hobbes;
- treating Confucian hierarchy as one-way obedience; and
- blaming only consumers when firms and institutions control information and infrastructure.

## What matters most

1. Week 3 is about **transfer**: use earlier theories on messy cases.
2. Separate actors and acts before choosing a theory.
3. Environmental labels and good intentions are starting points, not conclusions.
4. Compare full life cycles and realistic alternatives.
5. Acknowledge uncertainty, indirect stakeholders, future effects, power differences, and irreversibility.
