# Ethical Analysis of the Actions Described in Madam Kim's Interview

## Scope and factual basis

This analysis applies the Week 1–2 ethical theories to the facts reported in the supplied interview summary. It does not independently verify the historical claims or treat the numbered interview references as slide citations.

On the reported facts, the strongest overall conclusion is that the soldiers' sexual exploitation of Madam Kim—and any wartime government system that authorized, organized, or maintained it—was profoundly unethical. The later government's response requires more facts, but refusing meaningful acknowledgment or excluding survivors from a settlement would also raise serious ethical problems.

## Separate the acts before analyzing them

Do not analyze “Japan” as if it were one person performing one act. Distinguish:

1. Individual soldiers' participation in the sexual exploitation of captives.
2. The wartime government's creation, authorization, or maintenance of the system, if established by the case.
3. The later government's response, including acknowledgment, education, and compensation.
4. The negotiation and terms of the 2015 agreement.

This prevents the actions of particular soldiers, wartime officials, modern officials, and the Japanese population from being unfairly conflated.

## 1. Utilitarianism: overwhelming suffering

Utilitarianism asks whether the act produced the greatest balance of pleasure over pain for everyone. [W1, slides 47–54 and 58–62](<week1/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture One (1).pdf>)

The relevant stakeholders include:

- Madam Kim and other captive women;
- their families;
- affected communities;
- the soldiers;
- military and government officials;
- later generations affected by trauma, historical denial, and diplomatic conflict.

The soldiers or military might have received short-term benefits such as sexual gratification, military morale, or easier control of troops. Recognizing those claimed benefits does not endorse them; utilitarianism requires that they be included before comparison.

Against them stand extraordinarily severe harms:

- loss of liberty and bodily autonomy;
- repeated sexual violence;
- fear, humiliation, and physical injury;
- years of captivity;
- long-term trauma and inability to disclose the experience;
- suffering carried by families;
- continuing social stigma and historical mistrust.

Given the duration and intensity of these harms, the victims' suffering overwhelmingly exceeds any claimed military convenience or gratification. The system was therefore unethical under act utilitarianism.

### Rule utilitarianism

Consider the rule:

> Military forces may abduct and sexually exploit civilians whenever doing so benefits military operations.

The general acceptance of that rule would produce widespread fear, violence, insecurity, and suffering. A rule prohibiting sexual exploitation and protecting civilians would produce far greater welfare. The conduct was therefore also unethical under rule utilitarianism. ## 2. Kant: persons were treated merely as means

Kantian ethics requires:

```text
good intention
AND universalizable maxim
AND respect for persons
```

[W2, slides 3–13](<week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

### Good intention

The case does not provide every soldier's intention. Some might have acted for gratification, obedience, conformity, or fear of punishment. These intentions should not be invented.

Nevertheless, maintaining a system that provided coerced sexual access to soldiers cannot be justified merely by claiming military efficiency. Even if officials believed that it supported military operations, a supposedly useful goal does not cleanse an exploitative method.

### Universalizability

A relevant maxim would be:

> A military may abduct and sexually exploit vulnerable civilians whenever doing so serves its objectives.

This cannot rationally be accepted as a universal moral law. It gives powerful actors permission to destroy the autonomy and security of vulnerable people whenever exploitation is convenient.

### Respect for persons

This is the clearest failure. Madam Kim was reportedly taken as a child, held for years, and forced to serve soldiers. She was denied meaningful consent, freedom, and control over her body.

She was treated **merely as a means** to satisfy soldiers and serve military purposes, rather than as a person possessing dignity and autonomy. The conduct was therefore unequivocally unethical under Kant.

## 3. Ethical egoism: the strands expose different answers

Ethical egoism demonstrates why ethical theories can sometimes produce disturbing disagreements. [W2, slides 16–28](<week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

### Machiavelli

Machiavelli treats an act as right when it acquires or preserves the actor's power to dominate others.

The system plainly involved domination. If military officials believed that it increased control over soldiers, civilians, or occupied territories, a narrow Machiavellian analysis might classify it as ethical.

However, the long-term analysis may reverse that conclusion. Such conduct can generate resistance, international condemnation, diplomatic conflict, and lasting loss of legitimacy. If it ultimately weakens the government's power, it fails even Machiavelli's test.

More importantly, the possibility that domination could make such exploitation “ethical” reveals a profound limitation of Machiavellian egoism: it can treat the acquisition of power as morally decisive while disregarding the dignity and suffering of victims.

### Hobbes

Hobbes regards cooperation as ethical when it advances the actor's self-interest.

Officials and soldiers may have cooperated with one another, but Madam Kim's captivity was not voluntary cooperation. Calling coerced sexual exploitation “cooperation” would erase the decisive power imbalance.

Hobbes is therefore a relatively poor fit for assessing the treatment of the victims. It might explain strategic cooperation among members of the military, but it does not adequately establish that their exploitation of captives was morally acceptable.

### Ayn Rand

Under the course's presentation of Rand, ethical conduct must rationally advance long-term self-interest through uncoerced personal choice.

The system directly depended on coercion. Madam Kim's actions could not qualify as uncoerced choices. The soldiers' and government's conduct also created serious long-term risks to legitimacy, social stability, and international relations.

It is therefore strongly arguable that the conduct was unethical under Rand as presented in this module.

## 4. Aristotle: injustice, cruelty, and lack of moderation

Aristotle asks whether a voluntary act expresses virtue in the appropriate measure. [W2, slides 29–54](<week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

Participation in the system displays:

- **injustice**, because vulnerable persons were subjected to extreme and unequal harm;
- lack of **compassion**, because their suffering and humanity were disregarded;
- lack of **moderation**, because bodily desire and military objectives were pursued without proper restraint.

The voluntariness of an individual soldier's conduct may require additional facts. A soldier acting under a credible threat may possess reduced responsibility. However, duress could mitigate the judgment of that soldier's character without transforming the exploitation itself into a virtuous act.

Government officials who deliberately designed or maintained the system would have a much weaker voluntariness defence.

## 5. Confucianism: abuse of unequal relationships

Confucianism emphasizes reciprocal duties within social relationships. A superior possesses authority, but authority brings responsibilities toward those in a subordinate or vulnerable position. [W2, slides 55–68](<week2/Ethics - Lecture Slides - Lecture Two (1).pdf>)

The military possessed overwhelming power over captives. Instead of exercising authority humanely, it allegedly used that power to exploit them. This violated reciprocity, humane leadership, and the responsibility of powerful institutions to contribute to public good rather than merely pursue their own objectives.

A limitation is that the course's Confucian framework has a relatively weak doctrine of individual rights. Kant therefore provides a clearer explanation of why the violation of Madam Kim's personal autonomy is inherently wrong.

## 6. The later government response

Madam Kim's request shows that compensation and acknowledgment are not interchangeable:

```text
Money addresses material loss.
Acknowledgment recognizes wrongdoing.
Education addresses future understanding.
Survivor participation respects autonomy.
```

### Utilitarian analysis

A sincere acknowledgment and accurate education could:

- reduce survivors' continuing distress;
- restore dignity;
- improve historical understanding;
- reduce denial and misinformation;
- contribute to trust and reconciliation.

A government might argue that avoiding renewed controversy protects diplomatic stability. However, suppressing unresolved harm can also perpetuate mistrust. On the supplied facts, meaningful acknowledgment appears more likely to promote long-term welfare.

### Kantian analysis

If officials treat money as a way to make survivors disappear from public discussion, they risk using survivors merely as instruments for diplomatic closure. Respecting survivors as ends requires taking their testimony and demands seriously and allowing them meaningful participation in decisions that concern their experiences.

### Confucian analysis

Acknowledgment can be understood as fulfilling a relational duty to repair damaged relationships. Monetary settlement without sincere recognition may fail to restore trust or harmony.

## 7. Was the 2015 agreement unethical?

Madam Kim's opposition is morally important, but her opposition alone does not establish every feature of the agreement. A strong answer should ask:

- Were survivors meaningfully consulted?
- What acknowledgment or apology did the agreement contain?
- Was payment treated as a substitute for responsibility?
- Were survivors pressured to accept it?
- Did it address education and historical records?
- What consequences did it produce for survivors and relations between the countries?

The safest conclusion is conditional:

> If the agreement excluded survivors from decisions concerning their experiences and treated payment as a substitute for acknowledgment, it likely failed Kantian respect for persons and may also have failed to maximize long-term welfare. If survivors were meaningfully consulted and the agreement combined material support, acknowledgment, and educational correction, the ethical assessment could be more favourable.

## Overall conclusion

> On the facts provided, the soldiers' sexual exploitation of captive women and any government system that authorized or maintained it were unethical under utilitarianism, Kantian ethics, Aristotelian virtue ethics, and Confucian relational ethics. The immense and prolonged suffering defeats any plausible utilitarian defence, while the absence of consent constitutes a fundamental Kantian failure to respect persons. Machiavellian egoism might initially describe domination as self-interested, but this disturbing result exposes the theory's moral limitation and may be reversed by the long-term loss of legitimacy and power. The later government response should be assessed separately: compensation may be valuable, but it cannot necessarily replace acknowledgment, survivor participation, and accurate historical education.

The most compelling theories here are **Kant and utilitarianism**:

- Kant captures the non-negotiable violation of autonomy.
- Utilitarianism captures the vast scale, intensity, and duration of suffering.

## Exam-ready condensed answer

> The conduct was unethical under both utilitarian and Kantian ethics. Under utilitarianism, the soldiers' limited gratification or claimed military benefit was overwhelmingly outweighed by the victims' loss of liberty, repeated suffering, long-term trauma, and harm to families and communities. Under Kant, the maxim permitting military forces to sexually exploit captive civilians could not be rationally universalized, and the absence of consent meant that Madam Kim was treated merely as a means to military and personal objectives. Any government system authorizing the conduct therefore failed both welfare and human dignity. The post-war response must be assessed separately: if survivors were excluded and compensation was used as a substitute for acknowledgment, it would continue the Kantian failure to respect them as autonomous persons.
